Day 7: The Girl Who Clapped a Glass Over the Light

Thursday 5 July 2046, night

Lights off by twelve. You have school. There is jollof in the blue tub. —Ma note on the Nwosu fridge, Linden Rise

The rain came sideways off the river all night and let up only in short breaks over the old core.

At Linden Rise, Amara Nwosu had been calling up the staff and letting it go since ten, the copper at its head kept low. Around one something tapped at her window. It was the size of her thumb, soft-winged like a moth, and it clung to the outside of the glass with the rain running off it. It pressed its face to the pane and did not leave.

It had come because she called. It was hers. It had found her and nobody else, and she would decide who got to see it.

She had nothing to give it. It wanted feeding, and what these things ate came out of a cut, and she had never finished one. It pressed at the glass and waited.

She opened the window a hand's width and brought a glass tumbler down over it on the sill. The rim struck the metal frame and cracked from lip to base. The mascot went out under the gap and into the rain. The staff was still half-called in her other hand. Its copper jumped across the window and lit the whole room.

In the hall a door opened. Her mother's footsteps came down the corridor and stopped outside hers. Amber showed in the gap under the door. Amara stood with the cracked tumbler and the window open and water coming in over the sill.

Out on the fringe estates it was drizzle. At the Tangle the frames were battened and the polytunnels held. Hyejin Park lay down in the shed with the rain loud on the tin and slept through till light.

Natalie came back from the canal steps a little after five. She climbed the stair to Isabel's flat, went in, and turned the lock behind her. She had not gone down the steps. When she put her hands to the rail they would not take hold of it, and she had come back instead. Iz's coat and phone were still behind the bathroom door, where she had pushed it shut.

Under the old canal bridge Isabel stayed in the iron hatch all night. She did not call out. Nobody called to her. Her clothes had been wet through since the lane, and in the hatch they did not dry. Toward dawn the rain stopped and the yard went flat, and the birds came out along the arches.

She had counted it out: the lane to the steps, the steps to the yard, the yard to the arch, ten minutes at the outside. The whole night had gone by. Nobody had come down the lane. Nobody had come to the arch.

When it was light she tried to get out of the hatch. She was shaking so hard her teeth knocked. Her hands had stiffened past bending, and she could not work her fingers around the lip of the iron.
