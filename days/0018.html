Day 18: The Witness Who Kept the Second Name in Her Mouth

Sunday 15 July 2046, morning

Your witness will be contacted before the day. There is nothing more you need to do.a Continuity confirmation letter

The seam in the north lanes had closed crooked, and Elspeth Hale was still looking at it when the phone rang. She let it ring twice before she reached for it, because her fingers had not felt anything since the low crossing and she had to watch the hand go into the bag and come out with the phone. She was a hundred and twenty years old, stopped at seventeen, and the north lanes had no opinion about that or about anything else.

"Miss Hale? Ines Marchetti, Continuity. Is this a bad time?"

"It is a lane."

"I'll be quick, then."

"Go on."

The seam came out of the culvert mouth and down the bank and across the lane, green over grey, and where the blade had slipped the green had grown back over the parting and closed it crooked. Serrated tussock to the last blade of it, feeding on its own burn.

"I'm ringing to confirm you as the witness Constance Webb named. The sign-over is the twenty-fourth."

"I told Constance yes three weeks ago."

"Then it's confirmed. That's the good part." A page turned at the other end. "There's a second name on the file. It's an old file, and the office has to try."

"Which name."

"Natalie. Surname unknown. Old core. Last contact 2017."

The meltwater came down the lane and stood round her boots and did not touch the green. There was ash under the green. She had seen it on Friday and it had been grey then, and it was grey yet, which is what ash does when nobody finishes the work.

"Miss Hale? Do you know how to reach her?"

"I heard you."

"She's a witness of record. If she can't be reached, the office enters sought and not found. That's the form of words."

"And what number is on the file."

"There isn't one. There's a name, a district, and a year."

"Then it is a short file."

"It's a short file." The voice was young and it was sorry and it kept to the point. "You'd be doing me a kindness."

Elspeth looked at the hand that held the phone. She had watched it take the phone out. Now she watched it hold it, and it could have been a stone, a pen, a rail. The lane ran on under it and she felt none of that either.

"No," she said.

"You don't know where she is?"

"I didn't say that."

"Then—"

"I said no. Write what you like on the file. Sought and not found."

Behind the voice there was typing, or the sound of a room full of typing, and a screen throwing blue on a face she would never see. Her name in a box. It had been in boxes before.

"Miss Hale, I have to say it plainly, for the record. Precedent says a witness answers."

"Precedent says a witness answers. I know what precedent says."

"I have to ask again."

"You have asked twice and I have not answered twice. Write the third one down as the note, and let me keep it."

The pause this time was long. Then the voice came back smaller.

"I'll mark it sought and not found. I'm sorry to have troubled you."

"You have not troubled me."

"Goodnight, Miss Hale."

"It is morning."

"It's morning. Good morning."

The line clicked off. She brought the phone down and looked at it until the screen went grey, then put it away with both eyes on the hand the whole way.

She took the register out and set it on the flat of the lock-keeper's step, which was dry under the overhang. She stood the pen in her fist and found the blank line by looking, not by feeling, because a blank line and a written one were the same to her fingers now.

The Hundred and Fourth: Serrated Tussock.

She said it as she set it down, the way she said every entry, because an entry that is not said is not entered. Then the date. Then the walk-up: the outside stair, the door at the top of it, the woman who answered it. The refusal she wrote in the plainest words she had. The woman was asked to read the line aloud. The woman would not. The woman shut the door.

The pen went where she moved her arm. The letters came out cramped and leaned left, and every one of them was legible, and she could not have told by touch that the nib was on the paper at all. She watched the ink come. When the entry was finished she lifted the hand away and looked at the last word of it for a while.

Refused.

She read the entry back the way she read every entry back. Nothing in it was wrong. The lane went on running under the step, and the green went on over the ash, and the seam was still open where Isabel had left it. That would be a second entry. She would have to see it first.

At Linden Rise, Amara Nwosu stood in the cold by the drain lake and pulled up the Bay Sightings thread on her phone. Her fingers had gone numb, and she had to jab twice before the message opened. The unknown handle had answered the message she sent on Thursday, the one that said the culvert held more than she could cut alone. It did not say who they were. It asked where she had stood at the drain, and what she had seen there. Nobody asked that about a place they had only heard of. Whoever it was had stood at that water too.

The eastern wards were still frozen and Hyejin Park had the street to herself. She was forty-one, stopped at nineteen for eleven years and running again since, and the frost did not know that or care about it. Frost stood in the gutter and on the lids of the bins, and the low crossing was white and had nobody on it. She walked the ward with her hands in her pockets and her collar up, past the tram stop where the board still read SUSPENDED, and turned in at the house with the blue sticker on the letterbox.

It was the size of a playing card and it said This household is in good hands. Hyejin stood at the gate and read it twice. The daughter had pressed it on with two fingers squared, the way you'd set a stamp, and when she had finished she had looked up from the letterbox and said, "Seems a shame to have you break your back for grass."

The curtains were shut in the front room and in the room above it. There was nobody on the path and nobody at the glass.

Hyejin went past the gate to the far end of the front wall, where the brick was shoulder high and had been mended once with a paler brick and never painted, and she set her back against it and sat. From there her boots lay across the way of anyone who wanted the gate or the side path, and they would have to say something to her first. That was the whole of the plan and she did not dress it up.

The cold came up through the flags at once. She laid her hands in her lap and looked at them. She had washed them that morning and the backs had come clean, and the fine lines still held the dirt, which was the last of the plot and would keep. She left it.

The spade was round the back in the barrow, with the tape on the handle where it had split, the one she had brought from the Tangle eleven years ago. She could go round and lift it out and be gone before anyone saw her. She had left it there on purpose on Friday, and she was not going to undo a good leaving.

She ran the men in her head. A man in a fleece with a clipboard would come to the gate and say, Sorry, love, are you waiting on someone. She would say, I'm sitting. He would say, We're here for the garden. She would say, I know what you're here for. That ran well. She ran it again with a woman, and the woman came out worse, because the woman would be kind about it, and she had no answer that worked on kind.

Around noon she did the sum, because she had nothing else to do with her head. The twenty-fourth was eight days off. The van was not coming today and it was not coming tomorrow. She had known that when she sat down. She had sat down anyway, and the sum was going to sit here with her the rest of the day whether she liked it or not.

A man came along the terrace with a dog on a lead, and the dog stopped at her boot and put its nose to the leather, and the man pulled it on without a word. He had seen a wall and a pair of boots and had not put them together into a person. Nobody had walked round her. She had not needed to be walked round.

Once she got up, when a car came down from the top of the street. She stood with her weight off the brick and her hands out of her pockets and her knees stiff until it had passed and had not turned in, and then she sat back down. Standing any longer would have made the morning into a walk, and she had walked yesterday and it had bought her nothing.

Her back had melted the frost off the brick and the wet had gone through her shirt to the skin. She felt the line of it and stayed against it.

She took out the phone once. The screen came up blue and there was nothing on it, and she looked at the nothing for a while and put it away again.

In the afternoon the light went down behind the ridge and the kitchen window of the house came on, amber behind the net, and the telly started up. Hyejin sat through one programme and most of another. Twice the old woman's shape came to the glass and stood, and twice it went away, and the door stayed shut.

She did not knock. Eleven years of kettles and boots off at the mat, and now the path was eight feet long and she had nothing to carry down it that would not be an answer. The question had been asked on the back step, in a dressing gown, once, and kindly, and there was no second one coming unless she went and fetched it.

The lamps came on down the ward, one and then the next. Nobody had come. The brick behind her had gone from cold to only wet, and she sat against it with her knees up and her hands still, and the blue on the letterbox held the last of the light the way a chip of ice holds it, and the ward went dark around it.

Chronicle

Next morning, in the north lanes, a Continuity caseworker rang Elspeth Hale to confirm her as Constance Webb's witness for the 24th. Then, apologetically, she read the file's second name: Natalie, surname unknown, old core, last contact 2017. Did Miss Hale know how to reach her?

They had named her to stand for Constance, and the only other name on that file was one she had buried in 2017. Precedent said a witness answered. She could not, and could not say why.

Elspeth gave her nothing, so that Natalie's name would go no further than the file.

She could not feel the pen, so she wrote from the arm. Natalie would not read the line aloud; it could wait on her no longer. It came out cramped and legible: The Hundred and Fourth: Serrated Tussock. The walk-up, the woman, the refusal.

At Linden Rise, Amara Nwosu opened the Bay Sightings thread with numb fingers. The unknown handle had replied: where had she stood at the drain, and what had she seen? So the stranger had been to that water too.

In the eastern wards, Hyejin Park walked to the old woman's house with the blue Continuity sticker on its letterbox and sat down against the cold wall past it, so that whoever came for the house would have to move her.

Nobody came.
