Day 13: The Girl Who Let the Light Go First

Tuesday 10 July 2046, morning

Text me when you're in. Doesn't matter what time.a note on the Nwosus' fridge

The footpath ran out at a fence post with a council sign nailed to it, and past the post the field was water. Amara stood on the last of the gravel with the staff across both hands. The fog lay on the drain lake so low that she could see the tops of the brambles and none of their stems, and on the far side, under the ring road, the culvert mouth was a dark half-circle with the water sliding into it as if it had somewhere to be.

The buzz was in her back teeth. It had been there since she climbed the gate at the end of the close, thin and high, the way the fridge sounded at night once you had noticed it.

She had checked the arm in the bathroom before she left. Tie wound twice, knot on the inside of the wrist, sleeve down over it. It held. It would hold for small ones.

The first was at the edge, where the path went under. She knew how to find them now, which was more than she had known a week ago: you looked for the plant that leaned the wrong way. There was a ring of drowned nettles at the water line, and in the middle of it a clump of something flat and toothed that lay against the breeze and turned, very slowly, towards her shoes. Green, but too green, the green that came up on the burnt-out lots behind the precinct before anything else would.

She set her feet. She did not think about it, because thinking about it was how you got slow, and she had read that on three different forums and believed it on all of them. The staff came round low and short. Pale wood met the crown of the thing where the leaves came out of the root, and for the length of a blink a light ran up the grain towards her hands, copper, like the inside of a two-pence piece held to a lamp. The clump folded in on itself. The leaves went grey and soft and then were only leaves.

The buzz dropped a note.

Under the tie, something pulled. It was a seam drawing, not a pain yet. She kept her eyes on the water.

"One," she said, out loud, to nobody, and then made a face at herself for saying it.

The second was in the lake. She could hear it before she saw it, a knot in the sound off to the left where a hawthorn stood up out of the fog with its lower half gone. It would mean going in. She had been in the water at school, in the science block drain, and her shoes had not dried by lunch, and nobody had asked. Nobody had asked about anything.

She went in. The cold came over her trainers and then her ankles and then her knees, and it was not like getting into a bath the wrong way round, it was like being gripped. She breathed through her nose. The bottom was field, soft, with ruts in it where a tractor had been, and twice she stepped down further than she meant to and had to plant the staff to stay up.

The thing was in the roots of the hawthorn. A tussock, if it was anything, a fist of blades as thick as her wrist, rooted in the bark where nothing should root, and it was not turning towards her slowly. It was already turned.

She did not wait. That was the part she had got right last time and she got it right again: the staff up and over and down in one line, no wind-up, the end of it biting into the tussock where it met the wood. The copper went up the grain and this time into her palms, warm, and on into the wrists, and she held on through it because letting go was worse. The tussock split. Something in the roots let out a breath of air that smelled of struck matches, and then the hawthorn was a hawthorn with a wet scar in it.

And then it was quiet.

Not the low kind of quiet. The real kind. She heard a moorhen somewhere out in the fog, putting up a fuss. She heard a lorry change down on the ring road. She heard her own breath, quick and loud, and the water moving round her legs, and nothing in her teeth at all. Her ears felt as they did when you came up from the deep end, open and new.

Two, she thought. Two, clean, and the second quicker than the first. If it went on like this she could have the whole field done by the weekend. She could have it done and Mum could ask how her day was and she could say fine, and it would be true in every way that could be checked.

The moorhen stopped.

It started again from the culvert. Not from in front of her or from the left, not from anything she could see. From under the road, from the dark half-circle where the water went. It came up low, lower than the small ones had ever been, so low that she felt it first in the water against her shins and only then in her teeth. It did not climb and fall away. It came up and it stayed up, the way the hum of the towers stayed when you lay awake, except that this one had a shape to it, and the shape was wide.

She stood very still with the water round her knees.

The first one had been small. The second had been smaller, or had felt it, which she had taken for the same thing. She put the two of them side by side in her head, the clump and the tussock, and set them next to what was coming out of the culvert, and they did not reach. They did not reach together. They were not the same kind of thing.

The pull under the tie had stopped being a pull. She shifted the staff to her left hand and pushed her right sleeve back with her teeth, which was stupid, and the cold air got in, which was worse.

The tie was dark all along its length. Where it had slipped at the elbow she could see the line, and the line went down under the knot to her wrist and up past the crook of her arm, open, not deep but open, the whole way. Along the edges of it, faint, so faint she might have made it up, ran a thin colour that was not blood. It was the colour the staff had gone. It was the colour of the two-pence piece.

She pulled the sleeve down. It stuck at once.

In her blazer pocket the phone buzzed against her hip, and for half a breath she thought it was the other thing and her whole body went tight. Then it buzzed again, twice, short, which was Mum, because Mum always sent things in threes. She did not take it out. The screen would be blue and lit and full of words that each wanted an answer, and she could not get to it without taking her hand off the staff.

Out across the water the fog had started to thin from the top. She could see the ring road now, the barrier along it, the green sign for the junction. She could see the culvert better too. It was bigger than it had looked from the path. The water went into it smooth and black and did not come out anywhere she could see.

The sound did not go down. She waited for it to, the way she waited for a teacher to move on to someone else's hand, and it did not.

"Okay," she said. Her voice came out thin over the water. "Okay. Not that one."

She did not move towards it. She did not move away from it either. She stood in the drain lake with the cold climbing past her knees and her arm wet inside its sleeve, and she listened to the culvert, and the culvert went on.

By nine the fog had burned off the drain lake. Amara's reinstalled app showed her old Linden Rise post answered, the first reply in days. It came from a handle nobody knew: still there? looked bigger from the footpath this morning. The thread jumped to the top of Bay Sightings. On the eastern wards footpath, Hyejin Park's phone lit blue with it.

The phone lay in her palm with the thread open and blue, and she read it once.

still there? looked bigger from the footpath this morning.

The handle was five letters and two numbers. No name. No face on it. The sort a person made in a minute and threw down a drain the same day.

She read it a second time, the way you read a seed packet for the date.

Four rows over, Elspeth Hale set her trowel down in the dirt and sat back on her heels.

"You've stopped."

"I'm reading."

"Read with your hands."

Hyejin put her thumb on the side button and held it. Blue went out of the screen, and out of the wet clay between her knees, and she put the phone in her coat with the face turned in against her hip and picked up the trowel again.

Elspeth went back to her own bed. The register sat on the path by her boot, open at the spine, a pencil down in the crease.

"Bay Sightings," Elspeth said.

Hyejin did not look up. "What about it."

"It was on your screen. I read things upside down. It's a habit from the depot."

"It's a weather app."

"Weather's for farmers."

Hyejin turned a fork of couch grass and shook the dirt off the roots. There was a worm in the fork and she tipped it back into the hole and closed the soil over it with the flat of her hand. The phone lay flat against her hip, warm off her body, and did nothing.

"Culvert end's on the east page," Elspeth said.

"Then write it down."

"Somebody ought to walk it before the rain."

"Walk it, then."

"I walked it Tuesday. Water's over the ruts and up past the sign."

"Then it's up."

"The book says somebody checks it."

"The book says a lot of things."

Elspeth let that sit. She took the pencil out of the crease and turned it over in her fingers, blunt end and point, and put it back.

"There was a girl on the footpath Sunday," she said. "Down by the drain lake. Blazer, a staff with something on it, wrong coat for the weather."

Hyejin kept weeding.

"You know the one I mean."

"I don't."

A lorry changed down on the ring road, away off east, and was gone. Somewhere in the polytunnel a vent flapped, once, and settled.

Elspeth picked the pencil up and wrote something small at the bottom of the page and shut the book on it.

Hyejin worked the row to the end, root by root, and knocked the dirt off the trowel against the stone edging, and started back along the next one. The dirt was in the creases of her hands, old in some, new in others. She could feel where the phone was without touching it, a flat weight against the hip, and it stayed quiet, and she let it. She did not say anything to Elspeth. There was nothing on the east page that wanted her name on it.

Elspeth Hale took the register and set off from the eastern wards to walk the Linden Rise footpath herself. Hyejin watched her go and said nothing. Meltwater had taken the low crossing. Elspeth went in to the knee, and the cold got into her hands, and she turned back. At the wards she sat with the register open and could not feel the pen. The line stayed blank.

The shed was at the end of the garden, past the buddleia, with a corrugated roof that had a dent in it where a branch came down in the spring. Amara got the hasp up with two hands and a knee, because the padlock had swelled in the wet and the key wanted turning and lifting at the same time. Her fingers were not doing lifting. She got it in the end, and got the door to, and stood with her back against it until the shed came out of the grey and she could see the bench.

The floor was concrete. The cold came off it through her soles at once and went up her legs to meet the cold already there. Water ran off her trouser cuffs and made two small sounds on the concrete and then stopped making them.

She did not put the light on. There was a torch on the shelf and it wanted batteries she had taken for the remote in her room last week. She knew where the stool was without looking. She sat on it. The potting bench was in front of her, under the window, and she set the staff against the bench leg where it would not roll, and then she set the phone down on the bench with its face turned to the wood.

Her arm had stopped bleeding into the tie. She thought it had. She was not going to unknot it to find out, and it was not the knot that was the problem, it was that the sleeve had dried against the line and moving it now would open it again. So she sat with her right arm out a little from her body, held off like something carried.

The shivering had started somewhere on the footpath and had not stopped. It came in waves up her back and out through her shoulders and set her teeth going, and then it let her alone for a while, and then it came again. She had read on a forum that was what happened and that you should not get into a bath with it, that the change was too fast; you should get out of the wet things and into dry ones slowly, in a warm room. She was in a cold shed in wet things. There was no third thing to do.

Under the tie, up the whole length of her forearm, the line was live. That was the word she had for it. Live, like a wire the fence had caught, and when the shivering took her it pulled, and when she held still it did not pull, it only hummed. The colour at the edge of it she was not going to think about in a shed with the light going.

The phone buzzed.

It went against the wood, three short ones, close together, which was Mum, because Mum sent things in threes. She had been doing it since the flood warnings started coming and she had not stopped. The first would be about the road. The second would be about tea. The third would be a question, and it was the same question she had asked at four forty and five twenty and six, and it was not a hard question, it was where are you, are you dry, and there was no answer to it that did not have to be said out loud.

Amara looked at the back of the phone. It was a plain black case with a scuff on the corner from the science block stairs. Blue came out round the edges of it, thin, on the wood, and stayed a moment, and went.

The light in the window changed. It had been grey and flat and it went long and slant, and the water butt took a shadow, and the shadow climbed the fence and was gone off the top of it. A car went down the close and its lights came through the window and crossed the bench and were gone, and in the going they showed her the phone. Then it was dimmer than it had been.

She got up off the stool because her legs had gone stiff, and stood, and did not put her hand out to the bench, and sat back down.

The kitchen window came on.

It came on warm and low and it made the whole end of the garden into shapes: the buddleia, the canes, the water butt with its lid off. Amber on the path. She had not seen the garden from here in the dark before. It was a different garden. The shed stood in it and did not belong to it.

Through the window she could see the top of the fridge and the strip of wall where the calendar hung, and once, a shoulder and the side of a head going past, brown, with the hair in its knot, at the sink. Mum filled the kettle. The kettle took a while.

Amara put her hand on her sleeve and pressed the whole length of it down against the line, fingers flat, and held it there, and the shivering went up her arm into her chest and she breathed through it and did not make a sound. Out in the lane she had got the grate up and let the dark come at her and pulled back and run, and the water had been over her knees, and none of that would be a hard thing to say. I fell by the bike sheds. I stayed to help with the sandbags. I was at Hyejin's. Three. She had three.

She had had three at four o'clock as well. That was the trouble with them. They only held if you said them early, while the sleeve was still wet and the mud was still wet with it, and the heating had been on for hours on the other side of that wall and the damp in the cloth had gone stiff and grey at the edge and smelled of the lake. You could still fall by the bike sheds at eight. You could not still be wet from it.

The kettle clicked off. The kitchen went quiet in the way the kitchen went quiet, which was the quiet of a room that was not empty. Then a plate went down on the table, and then another, and then the chair legs moved, and the chair legs stopped.

The phone buzzed again. Only one, this time. Then nothing.

Amara turned her face towards the window and watched the amber, and the amber did not move, and the clock she could not see went on with whatever it was doing.

At some point the going in had been available for a while and she had been sitting here, and now the shoulder and the knot of hair had left the window and the plate had stopped going down and the amber had thinned along the bottom of the glass into something more like the colour of a lamp in a room that had been sitting a while.

The kitchen light went off.

Not the way a light goes off when someone turns it off at the wall. The hall went on first, blue-white through the gap under the door, and then the hall went off, and then the landing window came on above the porch, and that stayed, and that was the last thing in the house with anything in it.

Amara stood by the bench with her wet shoes on the concrete and her arm held off her body and the cold in her jaw, and watched the landing light. It stayed on for a long time. It stayed on longer than the morning alarm, longer than teeth, and she stood in the dark of the shed with the two of them doing the same thing in different rooms, and it stayed on.

The phone was face down on the potting bench and it was still there. She put her palm over the back of it and did not turn it over, and felt it warm under her hand, and took her hand away.

It was full dark outside now, and had been for a while. The light over the porch was the only thing left, and under it the path was empty, and the door was shut, and she stood in the shed in the dark and did not go in.

Chronicle

The morning after the flood, fog lay on the drain lake at Linden Rise until nine.

Before it lifted, Amara Nwosu made two more cuts there, alone, quick and clean. After the second the buzz went quiet for a breath. Then it started again from under the culvert, and stayed. What lay in there was bigger than both cuts together. Under her tie the line had opened wrist to elbow, copper along its length.

Her reinstalled app showed her old post answered, by a handle nobody knew: still there? looked bigger from the footpath this morning. The thread jumped to the top of Bay Sightings.

In the eastern wards Hyejin Park's phone lit blue with it. She read it, silenced it, and went back to the beds without a word to Elspeth Hale.

Not hers to answer. The warning had gone out, and a warning did what it did.

Elspeth Hale set off with the register to walk the Linden Rise footpath herself. Hyejin watched her go. The low crossing was under meltwater. She went in to the knee, the cold got into her hands, and she came back. At the wards she could not feel the pen. The line stayed blank.

Amara did not go in. Her phone buzzed with Mum again, and she left it face down on the potting bench. She sat in the unheated garden shed with her sleeve wet and watched the kitchen window go amber. The light went before she moved.
