Day 2: The Culvert Grate Where Amara Walked Out Ahead of the Staff

Thursday 5 July 2046, morning

Lost a cat near the Linden Rise culvert, grey with one white ear. Please ring before it gets dark.Handwritten notice taped to a bus shelter, Linden Rise

Natalie walked Isabel home along the river, the long way, with the water brown and high against the wall and the sky the grey of an old dishcloth. On the second-floor landing of Isabel's walk-up in the north lanes, Isabel got her key into the door and heard Hoshi before it opened.

The yowl came from the corner behind the desk. It was the same one as last night and the night before, low and long with a hitch at the end, aimed at the join of two walls where there was nothing but plaster and a curl of old tape. When Isabel came in, Hoshi stopped and looked at her. Then she turned back to the corner and started again.

"She's been doing that all week," Isabel said. "Day and night. Same corner."

Natalie stood just inside the door with her hands in her coat pockets. She didn't take the coat off.

Isabel went round the desk and put her palm flat on the plaster where Hoshi was looking. It was colder than the rest of the wall. Hoshi wound once round her ankle and yowled at her knee.

She filled the bowl, which Hoshi ignored, and put the kettle on, because the kettle was something to stand next to. Behind her she could feel Natalie looking at her, the way Natalie looked at a wall before she touched it.

She had worked out the sentence on the river path. Every blight that grew nerves went in Elspeth Hale's book on Garnet Street, with its ash in a jar, and for five years every jar of Isabel's had gone there in Natalie's hand. Last night Natalie had checked her root before she took her word for it. Every time Natalie took a thing off her she made it sound like a favour. If it was going to stop, it had to be said before Natalie could offer.

"I'm going to finish the north cut," she said to the kettle. "Tomorrow, probably. On my own."

"Okay."

That was too easy. Natalie said okay to things and then turned up anyway, at the end of the lane, with her hand out. Isabel got two mugs down. "And I'm taking the jar to Garnet Street. Me, not you. My name's never gone in the book."

Natalie looked at her. It went on a moment longer than a look needed to. Then she said, "What are you putting it in?"

"A jar?"

"Which jar."

Isabel opened the cupboard and held one up. "Pesto. It's finished."

"Wash it twice and get the label off. All of it, the glue too. She'll pick at it if you don't." Natalie took one hand out of her pocket and pointed. "And a lid that screws. Not one that clips."

"Is that a rule?"

"That's Elspeth."

The kettle clicked off. Isabel didn't pour. "So it's mine. The cut and the jar. You're not going to turn up at the end of the lane and hold your hand out."

"It's yours," Natalie said. "Both."

So there would be a step on Garnet Street, and her on it, and nobody in front of her to hand it to. Isabel poured, and slopped some, and wiped it with her sleeve.

The thing she wanted next she wasn't going to ask for. She talked about the trains instead. "It's the Kilbride line to Breen's Crossing, then a bus out to the ring road. I'll be back by four. Five, if the bus does the thing where it sits at the Centre for twenty minutes."

"Come and find me at the canal rooms when you're in," Natalie said. "Eight or so. The 14 stops outside."

"What's at the canal rooms?"

"Friends of the Tangle. I'm not going in."

"Great. Date night on the footpath outside a residents' meeting."

"There's an urn in there. I'll nick you a tea."

"Stolen tea."

"Council tea. It's not stealing, it's rates." Natalie took the mug Isabel held out and drank half of it standing, too hot, the way she always did.

She put the mug down on the desk, by Hoshi, and went. Her hand was back in her coat pocket before the door shut, holding something in there that crackled like folded paper.

Isabel ran the hot tap over the pesto jar and started on the label with her thumbnail. Behind her, in the corner, Hoshi started again.

Isabel came off the bus at the ring road just after ten, an hour out from her own lanes, and felt the ground at Linden Rise come up through her boots wrong. At home her line ran under her like her own stair in the dark. Here the pull under her feet was thin and young, laid over ten years of identical roofs, and it belonged to somebody else. It was like walking through a stranger's house with the lights off.

The drain lake was still over the path at the culvert. Amara Nwosu was waiting on the bench by the water with a box on her knees and a can from the school's vending machine going cold beside her. She was fifteen and looked it: school jacket, hood up, a satchel strap across her chest.

"Hi. You came." Amara stood. "I fixed it. The meter. Last night, at the kitchen table. It was the ground wire, and a joint, I think, so I did both."

"Cool," Isabel said, and meant it, and didn't know what else to say about a box.

Then Amara called her staff, and it was in her hands, pale green wood that smelled of sap from where Isabel stood, and the rest of her went with it. She was taller than Isabel. She looked twenty-two, twenty-three. Copper ran down both her forearms into the wood, and she stood in the wet with her weight easy on both feet, as though nothing anywhere was hurrying her and nothing ever would.

Isabel called her own. The staff came pale into her palms, the copper warm along her arms, the shape settling on her. She waded out until the water was over her ankles and the static came in at the back of her head, loud, a bad line with somebody shouting on the other end of it. It came from behind the grate where the reeds had jammed.

"Okay," Isabel said. "Stay at the edge. Two things first."

Amara stayed at the edge. Isabel was grateful for that, for about a second.

"Nerves first. If it's grown nerves, you take those off before you go anywhere near the heart."

"Why?"

"So it can't feel you coming."

"What does it do if it feels you?"

"Runs."

"Runs where?"

Isabel opened her mouth and nothing was in it. Natalie had said so it can't run, and Isabel had never asked where to. "Away. Deeper. Second thing, you never walk up on one alone."

"How close counts as alone?"

"It just. You don't do it."

"Okay." Amara nodded, once, as if she'd filed it. "But you're here. So I'm not alone." She stepped down into the water with the box held out in front of her. "Can I hold it to the grate while you watch? It's one joint. It should hold."

"Wait. I haven't told you how it goes yet."

"You told me. Nerves first, don't go alone. You're watching." She was already three steps out.

Isabel could have caught her by the sleeve. She thought of Natalie's hand closing on the staff, the coat, the key, and her own hand stayed where it was. If she grabbed this girl in front of the first thing she'd ever taught her, the girl would know Isabel didn't know what she was doing.

The needle in the box lifted. Amara took another step and it climbed past the felt-tip marks, past the last one, and hard against the stop.

Behind the grate, Isabel could see it now. A grey-brown mass the size of a car tyre was wedged into the mouth of the pipe, with the reed roots grown into its underside. Along its top edge stood a dozen pale threads, each about as long as a finger. They had all turned toward Amara.

Then the needle dropped back to nothing. Amara shook the box, and it rattled, and when she tipped it to the light Isabel could see the needle lying loose at the bottom, off its pin altogether.

The threads drew in. The mass slid back from the grate, a hand's width and then an arm's length, into the dark under the ring road, and the static went down to a mutter in the back of Isabel's skull. Where the reeds had been grown into it, their roots tore and floated up black.

Amara stood at the grate with the box against her chest. "It went higher than last time," she said. "Before it went. You saw it go higher."

"I saw," Isabel said. The water was cold through her boots. The blight was further in now than her staff could reach from the grate. Somewhere a train-line away, Natalie was doing whatever Natalie did on a Thursday without her.

Amara Nwosu stood at the culvert grate at Linden Rise with a dead box against her chest and her staff in her other hand, and the signal went behind her teeth like a held note. It hadn't gone. It had only gone back.

She had cut two on her own before this one, this winter, and told nobody, Isabel least of all. Neither of them had felt like this.

"Put that on the bank," Isabel said. "Then come back and stand where I put you."

Amara put it on the bench, upside down so the needle wouldn't fall out, and waded back. Isabel put her a body's length to the left of the grate, on the shallow side, and went in close herself, until her staff was through the bars into the dark of the pipe.

They waited. The water came to Amara's shins. Then the threads came back out of the dark, slowly, feeling along the pipe floor toward the light.

Isabel pinned the first one. Amara watched the copper run out of her arms along the pale wood and into it, and the thread go grey and still. That was nerves first. That was the rule.

Amara shifted her weight to her right foot, and the signal shifted with her.

She stood very still. The rest of the threads had turned, in the water, away from Isabel's staff and toward her. She moved her left foot a hand's width along the bank. The pressure behind her teeth slid that hand's width too. Isabel was right there with her staff in the pipe, nearer than Amara by a whole body, and they weren't looking at Isabel at all.

Nobody had said anything about that. Not Isabel, not the forums.

"They're following me," Amara said. "Not you."

Isabel's head came round. "That's not. They feel whoever's closest."

"You're closest."

Isabel didn't answer that. Her hands were shaking on the staff. Amara could see it from where she stood, the end of the wood ticking against the bars.

"Okay but what if I walk, along the bank, really slowly, and they come out after me, and you take them as they come? Is that a dumb idea? It might be a dumb idea."

"Natalie never said anything about doing it like that."

"Is it wrong, though?"

Isabel breathed out through her mouth. "Go slow."

Amara went slow. The threads came out of the pipe after her, one by one, into the grey light at the grate's edge, and Isabel took them one by one as they cleared the bars. Four. Seven. Ten. Her copper was the inside of a two-pence piece held to a lamp, and Amara felt her own arms warm in answer every time Isabel leaned.

The last one whipped. It came off the grate in a flick and went along the water to Amara's staff, and up it, and over her wrist. On its way back it went across the back of Isabel's hand where she gripped the bars.

It went grey under Isabel's staff a second later. The signal dropped to a single low note, far in under the road.

Amara looked at her wrist. A scald ran from the bone at the outside of it a thumb's length up her forearm, along her copper, about as wide as a shoelace, the colour of a new penny, wet. Across Isabel's raw knuckles ran a shorter one, the same colour, already weeping.

"The heart's under the road," Isabel said. "I can't reach it from here, and I'm not going in there. Without the nerves it can't feel us. It'll keep."

Amara wanted to ask how long keep was, and didn't. Isabel waded out of the water, slowly, and looked at her, and not at her wrist.

"Where'd you learn to stand like that?"

Amara had excuses ready, three of them, and every one was for the start of a thing. It was too late for any of them now. She looked at the water. "I read a lot," she said. "On the forums." It came out small.

"You didn't read your feet." Isabel looked at her a moment more. "You've stood at one of these before."

Amara didn't say yes. She didn't say no either, and Isabel nodded slowly, as though not saying no was an answer, and it was. So Isabel knew. Not the two, not where, but that her feet had done this before, and now every time Amara asked her something Isabel would be wondering what she already knew.

"Next time, tell me before you move," Isabel said. "Not after. Before."

That was all. Not that was right. Amara nodded and let the staff go, and her shape went with it, and the cold came straight through her school jacket. She was looking up at Isabel now, not down.

Isabel stayed as she was a while after, staff across her knees on the bench, copper still running faint down her arms. Then she let it go too.

"You're taller than me in it," Isabel said.

"I'm still growing, though," Amara said. "Out of it, I mean. I've gone through two pairs of school shoes this year. So I'll catch you up anyway."

Isabel said her bus went at the half hour.

Amara's phone went three times in her pocket. Mum texted in threes, and the third was always the question. Mum was at work till six.

Did you have lunch?

Chicken's defrosting in the sink, put it in the fridge by three please.

Where are you today?

Yes, I had a sandwich.

I'll do it now.

She left the third. Under it, further down the screen, sat three messages from a girl called Hyejin, who had written to her the night she posted: what it costs, how girls die, that nobody is coming. She had read them twice each. She hadn't answered those either.

She pulled her sleeve down over her wrist before Isabel could look at it.

Elspeth Hale opened the door of her house on Garnet Street before Natalie had knocked twice, and looked past her, down the step, to see who wasn't on it.

"Just me," Natalie said.

"So I see." Elspeth was in a cardigan nobody had made since Natalie was at school, and a skirt older than the cardigan. She looked seventeen. She had looked seventeen when Natalie first stood on this step, and when Natalie's mother was a girl. She stood back to let her in.

The front room was as it always was. The ledger lay open on the table, the last line in grey-brown ink: The Hundred and Third: Sagittaria. Beside it sat the ink stone, wiped and dry, and the steel nib on its rest, and a row of clean jars with nothing in them.

"I've had the stone out since Tuesday night," Elspeth said. "You posted it done."

"I posted the nerves done. The north cut's still open."

"Then I've had the stone out two days for half a job."

"A bit longer, then. Iz is closing it tomorrow. The jar's hers to bring."

Elspeth sat, and folded her hands on her knees. Natalie stayed standing. "I've never had her at my door."

"You will. I want you to wait for her." Natalie put her hand in her coat pocket and kept it there, off the letter, which was folded in three against the lining. "And if I turn up with that jar, send me home with it."

Elspeth's eyebrows went up a fraction. "That isn't how it's done. Whoever brings the ash."

"Then see that it's her that brings it."

Elspeth looked at the empty jars. "And when she's brought it," she said, "and I've written her, who does she bring the next one to, after me?"

"Not me."

"You've a steady hand. You know every girl in the Bay by her walk."

"I said no at Easter. It's still no."

"So you've said."

Natalie heard it land nowhere. She had heard it land nowhere at Easter, too. Elspeth's shoulders hadn't moved at all.

"My edges haven't been walked in a fortnight," Elspeth said. "Not by me. I know who's been walking them." She didn't say who, and Natalie didn't ask.

There was the itch, faint, somewhere off toward Hollis Street. Nothing much. Not hers. Natalie thought of the old core at dawn tomorrow, the grates and the lanes and the five-minute cuts, and of it some other morning with nobody on it at all, and only one somebody she could think of to put there.

"And how's your friend?" Elspeth asked, with the weight on friend.

"Teaching. Out at Linden Rise."

"The new growth." Elspeth never learned the names out there. "There's a girl out there three weeks found who hasn't come to me."

"That's the one Iz is teaching."

"Is it." Elspeth looked at her for a while, the long patient look she gave everything, as though it were a matter for the record. Then she got up, closed the ledger on its ribbon, and set the nib straight on its rest. "I'll hold the Hundred and Fourth. I'll not write it for anybody else, and I'll not take that jar from your hand. Tell her the step's here."

"I'll tell her."

At the door Elspeth said, "Mind the canal on your way. It's up."

"I know," Natalie said, and went down the step with her hand still in her pocket.

Isabel came back into the north lanes late in the afternoon and went straight to Kell's Lane without going home first, because if she went home first she'd sit down.

The lane looked as it always did: stacked windows, the fire stair down one side, a pram somebody had left under it. The static was faint. She had to stand still in the middle of the cobbles for a full minute before she found it, a thin bad line behind her ear that kept dropping out. On Tuesday night she'd walked off this cut half done and the opening had been right there under her heel. It wasn't there now.

She called her staff. Her arms complained, after the culvert. She laid the pale wood along the cobbles where she'd left the seam and felt for it.

It had closed over. Not straight, the way she'd opened it, but crooked, knitting back on itself across the lane and then off sideways, under the fire stair, toward the foot of the block wall. She followed it with the staff end, a step at a time, losing it and finding it.

"You'll catch your death, down there on your knees."

Mrs Vella had come out onto her back step, which gave onto Kell's Lane, with a basket of washing on her hip. She'd been on Linnet Lane forty years and she knew who was in and who was out and who wasn't eating, and she treated all of it as her business.

"I'm good," Isabel said. "Dropped something."

"It's been cold in this lane since Tuesday night. Colder than the weather. I said so this morning to anyone who'd listen." Mrs Vella pegged out a pillowcase. "The little ones from number nine play down that end. Should I be keeping them off?"

Isabel didn't answer yet. She'd lost the seam at the bottom step of the fire stair. To find where it really went she had to push into it, and she knew that, and she did it: leaned, and let the copper run out along the staff into the knit.

The seam pushed back. It came up the staff the way the thread had come up Amara's that morning and went over the back of her hand, over the scald she already had from the culvert. When she looked, the new one ran from the second knuckle to the wrist bone, as wide as a pencil, copper-coloured and wet, crossing the first.

She swore at it, quietly.

But she had it now. The true line went under the fire stair, along the foot of the wall for about four metres, and stopped at the downpipe. All of it would have to be opened again along its length and held while she went for the root. On Tuesday night it would have been ten minutes. Now it was an hour, maybe more, and all of it holding.

She could start now. It was light for another two hours. She had nothing left in her arms after the culvert, and she could feel the shape thin at her edges already, the way it went before it slipped. She held it anyway, a moment longer, crouched by the downpipe, before she let it go.

Tomorrow. After Natalie's round. She didn't look at why that was the time she'd picked.

"Keep them off the stair end," she said. "Just till tomorrow. The cobbles are lifting."

"The cobbles have been lifting since nineteen-ninety." Mrs Vella looked at her hand. Isabel put it in her pocket. "Come in and have a cup of tea. I've the kettle on and nobody to drink it."

"I've got to be somewhere at eight."

"It's half five."

"I've got to feed the cat."

"That cat," Mrs Vella said, "has been shouting at the wall since breakfast. I could hear it from the yard." She held the back door open with her hip and waited, with the empty basket, and did not go in.

Natalie was outside the community rooms by the canal when Iz came in on the 14, a little after eight. Behind the glass the Friends of the Tangle had the lights on and the stacking chairs out in rows, and somebody was standing at the front with a sheet of paper. Every pole on the street wore one of Hyejin Park's flyers, SAVE THE TANGLE over a hand-drawn vine, some of them three deep. Natalie had walked past twenty-one years of Hyejin and never once asked her anything. Down the line at the Exchange the Back Room was on, and she wasn't going to that either.

The canal was high from last night, black and quick against the brick. A little way along, past the lock, the patch by the bridge sat where it always sat, and pulled.

Iz came off the tram with her hand in her jacket pocket and her shoulders up round her ears. She hadn't slept much, and she'd used herself up somewhere today. Natalie didn't say so.

"No tea," Natalie said. "They've got a woman guarding the urn."

"Council security. Figures."

They stood under the pole. Iz told it in order, the way she told everything: the bus, the drain over the path, the girl on the bench, the box that went to the stop and then dropped its needle. The threads that turned. The girl walking the bank so they'd come out after her. The heart under the ring road. Natalie listened and looked at the water past the lock, and made herself look back.

"They followed her," Iz said. "Not me. I was closer. You never said anything about that."

"No."

"Is that a thing?"

"Not one I've seen." Which was true, and narrower than the question.

"And Kell's Lane's knitted over crooked. It goes under the fire stair and along the wall to the downpipe. It's an hour now, holding the whole way." Iz stopped. Her eyes had gone past Natalie's shoulder, toward the bridge, and come back. "You keep looking at the bridge."

"Water's up."

Iz waited, and when nothing else came she let it go, and Natalie watched her decide to.

"So tomorrow," Iz said. "After your round. I want you there. Watching. Not doing anything. Just there."

The itch was faint and far off and nothing to do with this. Natalie thought of a wall four metres long and Iz holding it open alone with nothing in her arms, and threads that went for the wrong girl.

"I'll come straight to Kell's Lane from the round," she said. "Seven, about."

"And watch."

"I'll take the stair end. You do the rest."

Iz held up her hand under the streetlight. There were two scalds across the back of it, crossing, both weeping. "You said it was mine. This morning, you said both."

"The jar's yours. I've told Elspeth. She won't take it off me if I turn up with it. She'll hold it for you."

"That's not what I asked."

"I know."

"So you'll watch me do three metres of it and then you'll do the bit that's hard."

"I'll do the bit under the stair, where you can't see the root." Natalie could hear herself, and couldn't make herself say the other thing. "Iz. Look at your hand."

Iz put it back in her pocket. Her shoulders came down, not the way they had this morning in the kitchen. "Seven, then."

"Seven."

The door of the rooms banged open behind them, and a man in a fleece came out with a roll of tape and a fresh stack of flyers. He taped one to the pole beside Natalie's head, over the old ones, and smoothed the vine flat with his thumb. The 14 came back round the other way, and Iz got on it without looking back. Natalie walked home by Weir Street, not the towpath, past a flyer on every pole.

Chronicle

Isabel travelled out to Linden Rise by train and bus, arriving just after ten. She met Amara Nwosu at the drain lake by the culvert, where Amara had repaired a faulty meter the night before. Isabel waded in to deal with a blight lodged behind the grate, and told Amara to stay at the edge.

Amara moved along the bank instead, drawing the threads out of the pipe after her, and Isabel took them one at a time as they cleared the bars. One thread lashed across the back of Isabel's hand as she gripped the bars, leaving a scald across her knuckles. Amara was also scalded, a thin mark running up her forearm from the wrist. The heart of the blight sits under the ring road and Isabel did not reach it. She said it could not feel them without its nerves and would keep. Amara admitted she had read about this on forums, and Isabel said she had been standing like someone who had done it before.

Isabel then travelled into the north lanes and went to Kell's Lane. A seam she had opened on Tuesday night had closed crooked, running under a fire stair and along the wall to a downpipe, about four metres. Working on it cut a second scald across her hand. She left it unfinished and said she would return the next morning.

Natalie walked the canal path in the evening and met Isabel outside the community rooms by the canal at about eight. Isabel described the culvert and Kell's Lane. Natalie agreed to meet her at Kell's Lane at seven the next morning, after her round, and to take the stair end while Isabel worked the rest. Natalie also said she had told Elspeth Hale that Isabel would bring the jar of ash to Garnet Street, and that Elspeth would hold it for her.
