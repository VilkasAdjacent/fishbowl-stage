Day 1: The Answer Given in Daylight on the Canal Bank

Wednesday 4 July 2046, morning

DO NOT USE THE TOWPATH AFTER HEAVY RAIN.Council notice, cable-tied to the railing of the old canal bridge

Amara Nwosu stood where the night's rain had pushed the drain lake over the path, under the ring road at Linden Rise, with the gutters still running cold off the concrete. The culvert grate breathed drizzle up through its slots, and the radio towers on the south ridge blinked red through the low cloud, one pulse, two.

She held the signal meter in both hands, the box of her own wiring meant to measure what her head read, the dial marked in felt-tip at midnight and the needle loose on its pin. It was half-built, the last joint soldered by lamplight, and the copper wire inside ran bare at one end where she had meant to add a ground but had run out of time.

The reeds that lined the lake were jammed hard against the grate, and where they met the water they had gone black at the root, black as the wet stain spreading under the ring road.

She wanted the needle to climb when she turned toward the metal, proof that the box read the signal the way her skull did. She wanted Isabel's answer to her first message on the phone before she went any closer to the grate, because she was afraid this one could feel her coming.

The signal was behind her teeth and at the back of her head, a pressure like holding your breath until the room tilted. She turned toward the grate. The needle jumped to the red mark and stuck there, trembling against the glass. She tapped it once. The needle fell to zero, then jumped again when she turned her head a fraction more, and this time it held.

She checked the phone. The screen came up blue and showed nothing from Isabel. She put it away, then took it out again when the drizzle found the gap at her neck, and still nothing. By the third check, the signal had climbed to the base of her skull, and she knew she was checking too fast, that the forums said wait five minutes for a reply, but she checked a fourth time, and her thumb had worn a warm circle on the glass.

She weighed the step. Closer to the grate, the needle might pin past the red, and she would have her proof, but the blight might feel her coming and run, or turn. Or she could stay on the path where the water lapped at her boots and wait for Isabel, which meant standing in the drizzle with the signal pressing at the back of her head. The forums said a girl needed data before she walked a parish, and they also said never walk another girl's ground alone. She had a staff in her bag, pale green wood that still smelled of sap, and two cuts done on her own, which made her a girl, which meant she should know better than to stand in a puddle counting seconds like a kid waiting for a text. She counted to ten, then made a face at herself.

She stood in the water that had covered the path until her boots were cold through, because the rain had not stopped, and the signal pressed at the back of her head, so that she could not tell if the pressure was the blight or her own pulse, until she turned toward the grate and the needle jumped to the red and she knew the box read what her skull read.

The gutters ran louder. A drop of water slid down her spine.

She turned toward the grate again. The needle jumped to the red and held. The signal pressed hard behind her teeth. She looked at the phone. The screen was blue, and there was nothing from Isabel.

The phone showed nothing from Isabel.

Across town, Isabel woke in Natalie's flat to an empty bed and the coat gone from its hook. Natalie had slipped out before her so that no one would ask where she was going. Isabel dressed and went out after her along the old canal, the gutters still running and the radio towers on the south ridge blinking red through the cloud.

Isabel walked the towpath along the canal, the drizzle finding the gap at her collar, her boots sinking into the mud where the water had overflowed, until the old bridge came up out of the grey morning, concrete grey against the black water, the radio towers on the south ridge pulsing red through the low cloud, and there on the bank above the water stood Natalie, her coat dark with rain, her feet planted at the edge, not looking back toward the flat.

Isabel stopped. Her knuckles were raw from the winter wind, and she flexed them, the skin tight and sore. She could call from here, which would give Natalie time to turn, to step back from the edge, to run. She could turn and walk back to the flat, to the empty bed and the hook with nothing on it, and wait for evening to learn whether Natalie would come home. Or she could climb the bank and make her look at her.

She climbed.

The concrete was slick with algae. Her boot slipped once, and she caught herself on the railing, the metal biting her raw knuckles. She climbed the rest of the way without stopping.

"Natalie."

Natalie turned, slowly. She looked at Isabel.

"You were leaving," Isabel said.

Natalie looked down at the water.

"Does it still stand?" Isabel asked. "Last night. In daylight."

Natalie looked at her for a long time. The drizzle ran down her face, darkening her hair against her skull. The water moved under the bridge, black and slow. The towers pulsed red.

"It stands," Natalie said.

Isabel made herself look at her face. She searched for the hesitation or the lie or the whole truth, and found only the calm she had worn for five years, the calm that had carried Isabel's ash to Elspeth every month. She could read nothing past the answer. Her chest went tight, a band pulling under her ribs, cold as the metal railing.

Then why the bridge, Isabel thought. Next time she would be awake before the coat left the hook.

At the grate, Amara wrote to Isabel a second time. She asked to be shown how to cut a blight that had grown nerves down to its root. She left out the two cuts she had already made there alone, because if Isabel knew, she would refuse. This time Isabel answered: she would show her when they could meet. Amara turned the phone face down on the wet stone.

Chronicle

The night's rain had pushed the drain lake at Linden Rise over its path and jammed the reeds against the culvert grate under the ring road. Amara Nwosu stood there with her half-built meter, a box for measuring the signal, the pressure blight put behind her teeth. The reeds had gone black at the root, and the needle jumped whenever she turned toward the grate. Isabel, whom she had asked for help, had not answered.

Across town, Isabel woke in Natalie's flat to an empty bed and the coat gone from the hook. Natalie had slipped out first, so that no one would ask where she was going. Isabel caught her on the bank by the old canal bridge. Natalie halted at her voice, because she had been seen.

Isabel made herself look at Natalie's face and asked whether last night still stood, in daylight.

"It stands," Natalie said.

Then why the bridge, Iz thought. Next time she would be up first.

Amara wrote again, asking to be shown how to cut a nerved blight down to its root. She left out the two cuts she had already made alone, so that Isabel would not refuse her. Isabel agreed to show her when they could meet. Amara turned the phone face down on the wet stone.
