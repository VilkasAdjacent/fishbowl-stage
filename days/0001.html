Day 1: The Answer Given in Daylight on the Canal Bank

Wednesday 4 July 2046, morning

DO NOT USE THE TOWPATH AFTER HEAVY RAIN.Council notice, cable-tied to the railing of the old canal bridge

Amara Nwosu stood ankle-deep where the night's rain had pushed the drain lake over the path, at the culvert under the ring road at Linden Rise. Water still ran off the concrete above her in cold strings. The reeds along the lake had jammed against the grate in the night, and where they met the water they had gone black at the root.

The signal sat behind her teeth and at the back of her head. That was how the blight felt to her: a pressure that got stronger the closer she came. It was strong now.

In both hands she held the meter, a box of her own wiring, the dial marked in felt-tip at midnight and the needle loose on its pin. It was meant to read what her head read. She had run out of time before the ground wire, and one end hung bare inside.

She wanted to see it work. She wanted to show it to Isabel, the girl who had replied to her post. Isabel would be impressed, right? And then she'd be able to learn how to cut cleanly, and what worked and what didn't, and she'd be good at it. Especially with this blight, whose pressure was nothing like the two she'd cut before. Sharper, like a migraine. She moved, and she almost felt something move with her. Was it watching her?

She turned toward the grate. The needle stirred, twitching. Did that count? She held the box out at arm's length, stretching, trying to be close to and far from the grate at the same time. The needle lifted, climbed, and then dropped completely. As she pulled it back, it stayed dead.

She shook the box. Nothing, just a slight rattle. She didn't have her screwdriver here, but she could already tell it was wire banging around. Probably a cold solder joint.

So the box worked, or near enough. She felt her grin coming through, and tamped it down. Be professional.

She checked her phone again. No response from Isabel. She waited, tapping her foot, and then the drizzle found the gap at her collar and she checked again.

She could go nearer, and find out what this blight did with someone standing that close to it. Or she could stay where she was, in water to the ankle, and wait for the girl who wasn't there, and might not show up at all. The forums said a girl didn't walk up on one that might have nerves, not alone.

She stayed. The cold came through her boots and into her toes. She counted to ten between checks, caught herself at it, and made a face. God, she was such a kid.

Still, nothing. She waited.

Isabel woke in Natalie's flat, expecting to find a weight behind her back and feeling nothing instead. The sheet on Natalie's side was cold, and when Isabel got up, she noticed the coat gone from its hook by the door. The mugs from last night were up on the bench, beside the sink. The keys weren't in the dish. Natalie had them, then. Probably.

She stood and looked at the couch for a moment too long. Last night Natalie had told her about the ditch under the old canal bridge, and stopped halfway through, and Isabel did not have to think about where to look. She dressed quickly, grabbed her phone, and left the flat.

She went out along the towpath, the damp of yesterday's jacket chilling her. The canal had come over the path in places and the mud squelched around her boots. The old bridge came up out of the drizzle, and below it, halfway down the bank on the wet grass, Natalie was going carefully toward the black water. She hadn't looked up yet.

Isabel stood for a moment, weighing. If she stayed here, Natalie would keep going. If she called out, Natalie might stop. But then they'd have to talk.

"Natalie."

Natalie stopped. For a moment she stood as she was, one foot lower than the other on the slope. Then she turned and came back up, and stood on the path next to Isabel, close but not.

Isabel made herself look at her face. "What are you doing out here?"

Natalie turned and looked back at the flood-swelled canal running past. Water ran off her hair and down her jaw, and she didn't wipe it. There was something in the way her eyes stared, Isabel thought, but she couldn't describe it. As the moment stretched, she gave it up. "You don't have to tell me. Sorry."

"It's okay. It's not something I'm ready to talk about just yet. You'll have to be patient with me on that. Okay?"

"Okay." A beat, and her stomach grumbled, which set off a small giggle from Natalie, and then the two were laughing, breath fogging in the air.

"Come on, let's go back to mine," Natalie said. "I'll do eggs. Proper ones."

They went up past the bridge, where a council notice had been cable-tied to the railing, laminated, gone cloudy with rain. DO NOT USE THE TOWPATH AFTER HEAVY RAIN. Natalie read it out, flat as a newsreader, and looked down at the water over both their boots.

"Bit late," Isabel said, with a smile. "Did you take the keys? I didn't lock up. I couldn't find them."

Natalie patted her coat pocket, and it jangled. "Always."

"Okay. Good." Isabel looked ahead along the towpath, where it went under again past the lock. "We should cut up by the garages. It'll be drier."

"You've thought about this."

"I try," Isabel demurred. Natalie made a small sound that might have been a laugh, and let her lead.

It was always easier to talk to Natalie, and she wanted to figure out why, but maybe not this morning. Not after how hard it had been last night. This morning, she would just enjoy it. Or try to.

She felt a buzz in her pocket, the girl from the forums. Right. She tapped out a quick reply, tossed the phone back in her pocket, and looked up as Natalie asked "Who's that?"

"The girl from the forums. She wants me to teach her."

Natalie didn't say anything for a few steps. "You going to?"

"Someone has to." Left unsaid was whether she could. Isabel knew she could. It didn't need saying.

The silence grew legs and shadowed them. As they stopped at the next traffic light, something simmered in her, and a question burst out.

"I need to know. Was last night real? To you?"

A breath, and then another. "It's real," Natalie said.

Isabel shifted, her hands unclenched. Then she looked for the rest of it in Natalie's face, the catch or the lie or anything at all, and found the calm Natalie wore on a cut, and nothing past it.

Then why leave? Why the bridge? Next time she would be up before the coat left the hook.

At the grate, Amara went to message Isabel again.

> Can you show me how to cut today? I've done two already, but this one's different.

She backspaced the second part out, and tapped send. If Isabel knew, she might say no, she might think Amara was getting ahead of herself.

This time the answer came inside a minute.

> sure. when do you want to meet? after 10

Amara replied with a time and place before she could think, and retreated to a bench, phone in her pocket. It was damp, and she sat anyway. Not like she was getting any drier today.

She pulled out the box again, turned it over in her hands. She had been excited to show it to Isabel; barely started and already she was doing things that no one else had done. But now she had no proof. She tossed it back in her satchel.

She ran through the questions she wanted to ask. She wanted to be sophisticated, didn't want to be asking beginner questions. "How often do you walk your rounds?" she asked the air. Or, perhaps, one from the forum, "How long do you give a root before you call it dead?" No one was around to hear her pondering, it was a miserable day to be outside. "How close can you get before it notices you?" Nah, too basic.

A yawn forced its way out of her tight jaw, and Amara remembered that vending machine outside the school. She meandered over and grabbed a can of hot coffee, making a noise at the bitter taste. At least it was something warm to hold, and eventually she'd get used to it, she supposed.

She wandered back to the bench to wait, hands warm and eyes open.

Chronicle

The night's rain had pushed the drain lake at Linden Rise over its path and jammed the reeds against the culvert grate under the ring road. Amara Nwosu stood there with her half-built meter, a box for measuring the signal, the pressure blight put behind her teeth. The reeds had gone black at the root, and the needle jumped whenever she turned toward the grate. Isabel, whom she had asked for help, had not answered.

Across town, Isabel woke in Natalie's flat to an empty bed and the coat gone from the hook. Natalie had slipped out first, so that no one would ask where she was going. Isabel caught her on the bank by the old canal bridge. Natalie halted at her voice, because she had been seen.

Isabel made herself look at Natalie's face and asked whether last night still stood, in daylight.

"It stands," Natalie said.

Then why the bridge, Iz thought. Next time she would be up first.

Amara wrote again, asking to be shown how to cut a nerved blight down to its root. She left out the two cuts she had already made alone, so that Isabel would not refuse her. Isabel agreed to show her when they could meet. Amara turned the phone face down on the wet stone.
