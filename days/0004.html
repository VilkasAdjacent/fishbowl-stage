Day 4: The Stall Woman Gave a Girl a Tomato and Asked Nothing

Saturday 7 July 2046, morning

Keep off the drains on Linnet Lane. Ask before you go down.Handwritten note pinned inside the lobby of Kell's Lane block, 4 Kell's Lane

Amara Nwosu came off the tram at the top of Lockyer Street a step behind Mum, with the satchel strap across her chest and the meter inside it, and the dressing on her wrist under two sleeves. It was Saturday. Mum worked for Second Growth, which wanted to build its hall on the edge of the Tangle, and she was walking the site once more before Tuesday's consultation. She had brought Amara rather than leave her at home on Rowan Crescent with an arm she didn't trust. Her lanyard was on, though nobody was paying her today.

Lockyer Street ran down toward the canal between old warehouses with flats in them. Halfway down, a white van was parked half on the kerb with the Second Growth leaf on its side. As Amara passed it the satchel swung hard against her hip, out and back, twice, though she hadn't changed her step. She put her hand flat on the flap and kept walking. The signal behind her teeth stayed quiet the whole length of the van, and quiet after it, and she looked back at the leaf on its side door until Mum said her name.

At the bottom the wall began, long and brick, with trees standing up over it much too big for a park. There was a flyer on every pole, SAVE THE TANGLE over a drawn vine. By the gate a stall had its shutter up and its trestles out, and in the window behind the tomatoes sat a council notice about the hall, curling at the corners. A woman with greying hair tied back in green garden twine was weighing tomatoes for an old man in a cap. Amara had bought tomatoes from this stall last summer, a whole bag, while Mum walked the site with her plans. She'd been a different person last summer. She hadn't owned a box.

"Can I walk to the end and back?" she asked. "You'd see me the whole way. It's straight."

"To the gate," Mum said. "Where I can see you without turning round."

"The gate's right there."

"Then it won't take you long."

Mum went up to the stall with her meeting face on, the friendly one. "Morning. You'll be Hyejin Park. Your name's on every pole from the tram."

Amara stopped with her hand on the satchel buckle.

Hyejin Park. Three messages, the night Amara had posted about the culvert: what it costs, how girls die, that nobody is coming. She'd read each one twice under the duvet with the brightness all the way down, and answered none of them, because answering would have made them true. She'd pictured someone old and certain, in a dark room somewhere. Not dirt in the creases of her hands. Not weighing tomatoes for a man in a cap.

She looked at the trestle, and the scales, and the kerb, and anywhere that wasn't Hyejin's face. Hyejin didn't know her face. There was no reason she would. Amara made herself breathe through her nose, slowly, the way she did before an exam.

"And you're the hall," Hyejin said.

"I'm Chioma Nwosu. I'm running Tuesday evening. I wanted to see it in daylight before I stand up in front of people who love it."

"Then tell me in daylight. Why here? There's the old gasworks. There's half of Corrigan standing empty."

Amara went along to the gate, not fast. It was iron, painted green a long time ago, and the trees inside came right up to it. She turned her back to the stall, so her body was between the box and Mum, and took the meter out and held it low against her side.

The needle sat where it always sat now, a hair above zero, where the pin had bent. She tipped the box toward the gate. The needle lifted past the first felt-tip mark, and on, slowly, to the second, and stayed there. She turned it toward the wall. It dropped a little. Back to the gate, and up to the second again, steady, not jumping as it had at the culvert.

Two marks, less whatever the bent pin added. She'd only ever measured that at rest. Out here it could be anything, and she wanted it to be real so badly that she didn't trust herself to read it.

"The canal water," Mum was saying behind her, "and where the grid comes in. You'll have all of it Tuesday, with the maps."

"I've seen the maps."

"Then you've seen why."

"I've seen a car park drawn over my beds."

Amara knew this one. Mum did it at parents' evenings and at Aunty Ngozi's, when the conversation went somewhere she didn't want it: she agreed with the part that didn't matter, and moved on to the bit she'd chosen before she arrived. She'd say Tuesday again in a minute. She'd say it so kindly that Hyejin would have to be rude to push past it.

"I'd rather say it once, properly, with everyone there," Mum said. "If I give you half of it at a stall, half is what goes round."

"Have you been in? Past the gate? Have you put your hand in those beds?"

"I have. It's remarkable. I'm not going to stand up Tuesday and pretend it isn't."

There. Tuesday, and kind with it. Hyejin had a bunch of string in one hand and she pulled it tight round nothing.

"We'll have three hundred names on Tuesday," she said. "Three hundred, from this street and the next two."

"Good. Bring them all. I mean that."

Amara glanced back. Mum had her notebook open on the end of the trestle, and she was writing 300 with a line under it. Hyejin had meant it as a threat, and Mum was writing it down as a number of chairs. Amara felt sorry for Hyejin for a second, and then remembered who Hyejin was, and didn't know what to feel.

She moved a step closer to the bars. The needle went no higher. One more, from the corner where the wall met the gatepost, and she'd know if it was the gate or what was behind it.

"What's she reading?"

Hyejin's voice had changed direction. Amara's head came round before she could stop it. Hyejin was looking past Mum's shoulder straight at her, at the box, and then lower, at her left wrist, where the sleeve had ridden up off the edge of the white dressing.

Amara's whole skin went hot. She couldn't get the box down. Her hand had forgotten how.

"Oh, it's a thing she built," Mum said. "She points it at everything. We're out at Linden Rise, and she's had it at every drain on the estate."

Mum. Linden Rise, out loud, across the tomatoes, to the one person in the Bay who had written to a girl at Linden Rise and got nothing back. Amara watched Hyejin put it together: the estate, the box, the wrist. The girl who hadn't answered. If she said it now, she'd say it to Mum. If she didn't say it now, she could say it to Isabel, or say it on Tuesday in front of three hundred names, any time she liked, for as long as she liked.

Amara put the box back in the satchel and got the buckle done on the second go. "It's for physics," she said. Her voice came out too high. "Interference. It's not very good yet."

"Mm," Hyejin said.

She picked a tomato off the top of the pile, the reddest one, and held it out over the trestle. Amara had to come and take it. She didn't see how not to. It was warm, though the morning wasn't.

"On the house," Hyejin said, to Amara and not to Mum. "Grown just there. You'll be at the back on Tuesday, I expect."

Mum paid for a kilo and said thank you, and took Amara's elbow, the good one, and they walked back up Lockyer Street past the van.

Amara held the tomato in her right hand the whole way to the tram. Grown just there, behind the gate the needle had climbed for. Hyejin hadn't said a word about the messages. She'd said you'll be at the back, as if she'd already put Amara somewhere, and she'd given her a thing from inside the wall to carry home in front of her mother. It might have been kind. It might have been the opposite of kind. Amara turned it over on the tram, and turned it back, and couldn't decide whether she was meant to eat it or keep it, or what it said about her either way.

Isabel came back to Kell's Lane at midday with the pesto jar in her jacket and her right hand in her pocket, and found Mrs Vella already at the downpipe in her coat, holding a key on a loop of string.

The static was fainter than yesterday. Isabel had to stand still at the drain grate at the foot of the downpipe with her eyes shut before she found it at all, a weak signal behind her ear that cut out for five seconds at a time. Along the foot of the wall the seam she'd opened and lost had closed over again, crooked, the mortar knitting back in a line that wandered off from where her staff had run. Yesterday it would have taken an hour to open. Today she wasn't sure she could find enough of it to start.

"That's the cellar," Mrs Vella said, and held the key up. "The block's. I've had it since the caretaker died in oh-three, and nobody's ever asked for it back."

"Can I borrow it?"

"You can tell me first whether the little ones from number nine are safe. I've rung the council three times now. The girl on the phone said someone would come and look at the drains. Nobody's come and looked at the drains, love, except you."

"They're safe if they keep off them."

"That's not safe, that's careful." Mrs Vella turned the key on its string. "In ninety-eight they had this lane up seven weeks. The old drain's brick, under Linnet Lane, from when the lanes were laid. They ran the new pipe from the downpipe under the block and joined it on to the brick one, under the cellar floor, and that joint never sat right. Every winter since, that cellar's smelled. The Grech boy at number four was poorly the whole of that year and nobody could say with what." She held the key out. "You come up and tell me what you find. Whatever it is."

"I'll tell you," Isabel said, and took it in her left hand.

The cellar door was round the side of the block under the fire stair, and the steps went down steep into a smell of wet brick. Mrs Vella stayed at the top with her hand on the rail.

At the bottom there was a bulb that didn't work, a row of meters, three bins and a pram with no wheels. Isabel stood on the concrete. Natalie was on Weir Street, ten minutes away. If Isabel went and got her, Natalie would come down these steps first, and it would be Natalie's root again, and Natalie's jar, and Isabel holding the torch. She called her staff.

The pale wood came into her hands and the copper ran down both forearms, and the shape settled over her.

Her right hand hadn't closed past halfway since yesterday morning. She'd meant to hold her phone in it for the torch. It wouldn't hold the phone and the staff both, and the phone slid down her fingers on to the concrete, so she left it there face up, throwing a white circle on the ceiling, and worked by what came back off the walls.

The static came up through the floor, or seemed to. The crooked knit was in the floor too, a line where the damp wouldn't sit, and she followed that with the staff end, a hand's width at a time. It ran across the slab from the side nearest the lane toward the back wall, the Linnet Lane side, wandering left of where any pipe ought to go. Twice the static dropped out altogether and she stood in the dark with the staff on the concrete and waited for it, and twice it came back fainter, from nowhere she could point to.

By the back wall there was an inspection cover set into the floor, cast iron, the size of a tea tray. The knit ran straight to it.

She put the staff in the crook of her left arm and got her fingers under the edge of the lid. It was heavy. She got it up a hand's width with her left hand and the heel of her right, and looked under.

The brick drain ran across under the lid, and on the joint where the new pipe met it there was a dark stain on the brick, warm, the air over it moving a little. Nothing else. Whatever had sat there had gone.

Then the static came, loud, from behind her.

Not from the drain. From the lane side of the cellar, under the bins, the way she'd come in. Between her and the stairs.

She turned too fast. The lid came out of her hands and down into its frame and took the first two fingers of her right hand with it, iron on iron on knuckle. She heard herself make a noise. The static went up, and up, louder than it had been all week, so loud it wasn't behind her ear any more but in her teeth, and she was on one knee with her hand trapped and the staff sliding off her arm and she thought, very clearly, this is why. Never walk up on one alone. Natalie had said it the first night, and never said why, and here was why, under the bins, coming.

She got her fingers out. She got the staff up.

The static was already going. From loud to far, down and along under the side wall, and halfway it dropped out and didn't come back. Toward Linnet Lane, or back under Kell's Lane. It hadn't come at her. It had gone round her.

"Fuck," Isabel said, to the cellar. "Fuck, fuck."

Her hand was hot under the gauze and getting wet. She held it against her stomach and breathed through her teeth until she could think past it, and what she thought was worse. It had sat under this block for two days with nobody near it, and it had waited for her to come down the stairs before it moved. A root with no nerves didn't do that. She had taken every nerve it had on Tuesday night. It had felt her crossing a cellar floor in the dark, and let her get all the way to the far wall with her back to it.

She stood with the staff until she was sure there was nothing to hear. She let the shape go, and the cold came in through her jacket. Picking her phone up off the floor took her three goes with the left hand, and she swore at it every time. When she went up the steps she held the rail with her elbow.

"Well?" Mrs Vella said.

"I don't know where it is," Isabel said. "The smell. It was here and it isn't. Keep them off the drains on Linnet Lane as well."

Mrs Vella looked at her for a while, and at the gauze, which had gone brown at the knuckles. "That's my side."

"I know."

"Then you'd better come in and have a cup of tea and tell me what that means," Mrs Vella said, and held out her hand for the key, and waited.

The rain came back to the old core in the middle of the afternoon, and by four Natalie was out in it, going down to look at the long grate under the tram stop. The towpath was the quick way, and she didn't take the towpath with the water up. She went round by Lockyer Street instead, along the Tangle's wall, which she hadn't walked the length of since spring, and came round the corner of the stall before she'd thought about whose stall it was.

Hyejin Park had it half packed in the wet, crates of tomatoes stacked under a tarp, the council notice in the window gone soft and dark at its corners. She was folding a trestle. She looked up, and for a second neither of them did anything at all.

"Nat."

"I'm going to the tram stop," Natalie said.

"I can see where you're going." Hyejin got the trestle flat and leaned it on the wall. "Your Second Growth woman walked the site this morning. She brought her girl. The girl had a box."

"She's not mine."

"The girl's from Linden Rise." Hyejin pushed wet hair off her face with her wrist. "I wrote to a girl at Linden Rise."

A box with a needle that went to the stop. Iz had told her all of it under the pole on Thursday night. If Natalie walked on now, Hyejin would know that she knew. She stopped.

"I'll take some beans, if you've any left."

Hyejin found half a bag under the tarp and didn't weigh it. She held it out, and when Natalie reached for it she didn't let go straight away.

"Come in and see the beds. Five minutes. The rain's nothing under the trees."

The canal was up. Natalie could hear it past the end of the wall, louder than this morning, and she knew without looking how high it would be against the stone at the bridge, and where the hatch was, under it, and that it would be pulling. Hyejin had come round the end of the stall to the gate. From there she could see along the towpath to the bridge as well as Natalie could.

Natalie kept her eyes on Hyejin.

"Twenty-one years I've walked past this gate," she said. "I'm not starting today."

"Then don't start. Just come in the once." Hyejin had the twine come loose at the back of her hair. "They'll pave it inside a year, Nat. Then you won't have to walk past it ever again. You'll have that."

"Don't."

The pull came stronger, low in her, like the slope of a floor that tipped toward the water. Her head wanted to turn. To give her hand something to do instead, Natalie put her right palm on the gate. She had a scald across the base of that palm, over the callus, from the grate in Salter's Lane, wet and soft in the middle. The bar was cold and wet. She held it, and kept her face on Hyejin's, and pressed, and the soft middle of the scald tore open against the iron. It hurt cleanly. It was a thing to hold on to that wasn't the bridge, and she kept holding.

Hyejin put her hand on the gate too, a bar along.

"I was on that bridge," she said.

The rain ran off the end of the stall. Hyejin's hands had gone still on the bar, gardener's hands, dirt in the creases.

"I know where you were," Natalie said. "I'm going to the tram stop."

She took her hand off the gate. There was copper-brown on the green paint where her palm had been, a smear of it, and the rain started on it at once. Hyejin looked at the bar and then at the hand.

"What's that?"

"Salter's Lane. The frost had it in the iron."

"You want that wrapped."

"I've stuff at home." Natalie put the hand in her coat pocket and her fingers found the letter there, and she moved them off it. "What do I owe you?"

"Nothing."

Natalie got her phone out left-handed and held it to the reader on the end of the trestle anyway, until it chirped. Hyejin's pocket buzzed. She didn't take it out. She turned her back and went on pulling the tarp over the crates, tucking the corners under, one and then the next, harder than the job needed.

When Natalie got to the tram stop, the grate was under four fingers of brown water. She did it from the kerb side, kneeling, with the staff in her left hand, and her right in her pocket, bleeding into the lining.

Isabel came up the stairs on Weir Street a little after eight with the rain still on her jacket. Natalie had the door open before she knocked, and the kettle was already going.

Natalie's boots stood on newspaper by the heater, toes to it, and the narrow strip of amber from the baseboard runner lay across the floorboards between them. Her right hand had new gauze on it, round the palm and between the thumb and finger.

"What happened?"

"Gate," Natalie said.

"What gate?"

"The Tangle's."

Isabel looked at her. "You went in the Tangle?"

"No." Natalie filled two mugs left-handed and slopped one. "Sit down. You've been at the lane."

Isabel didn't sit. She gave it to her in order, because she'd been putting it in order all the way over, on the tram, so it would come out without her voice doing anything: the key, the cellar, the cover, the stain. Then the static, behind her, by the door.

"It waited," she said. "Two days on its own under there and it waited till I was across the floor. Then it went round me."

Natalie went still, as she did over a grate before she said get back. "Which way?"

"I don't know. I lost it." She held up her hand, the gauze brown at the knuckles. "Lid came down on this."

"You went down there on your own."

"Mrs Vella was at the top of the stairs."

"Leave Kell's Lane, then," Natalie said. "Till I can come with you."

Four days she'd been fighting for that lane. Isabel looked at the stain on her gauze and thought about the static in her teeth, and found she didn't have it in her tonight to fight for it.

"Okay," she said, and hated how easy it came.

"Hyejin says the Second Growth woman's girl had a box at the Tangle this morning."

"Amara?" Isabel put the mug down. "Her mum's Second Growth? She never said." Of course she never said. Amara said three things and knew forty. "I'm at Linden Rise tomorrow, ten, at the culvert. She wants me to watch her hold the box to the grate."

"Good."

Natalie sat down on the arm of the sofa with her mug, under the tall glass, the radio towers blinking red on the ridge behind her in the rain. "Salter's Lane," she said. "If the thaw comes Tuesday, it's five in the morning. It's yours. I won't be there."

"Where will you be?"

"Here. In bed, if I've any sense."

"So I get your grate, on my own, with a hand that doesn't close, and you get my lane."

"The lane's got a root that can feel you coming. Salter's Lane is a grate you've watched me do fifty times."

"And the Friday after? The chapel yard? The tram stop?"

Natalie drank some of her tea.

The coat was on its hook by the door, olive and long. All week Natalie's hand had gone into the inside pocket of it and come out empty.

"What's in your pocket?"

"A letter."

"From who?"

"Home."

"Port Ellery." Isabel waited, and nothing else came. Five years, and Isabel knew the name of the town and that Natalie didn't go back to it, and that was all. "Is that Tuesday? Nobody plans a grate four days out, Nat. You don't. You go when it's thawed."

"Tuesday's Salter's Lane. If it thaws."

"You can't tell me, or you won't?"

It came out too loud for the flat. Natalie didn't answer that either, and Isabel heard herself go on, and couldn't stop. "My name's never gone in the book. Not once, in five years. Every jar's gone up that step in your hand. And now I get your round, with your letter in your coat, and I'm supposed to take that as a present."

Natalie put her mug down on the floor by her foot. She got up and came over and reached for Isabel's right hand, the bad one, to look at it.

Isabel pulled it back against her chest. "Don't."

Natalie stood with her own bandaged hand still half out, and then let it drop.

The rain went sideways across the glass. Natalie took Isabel's mug, which was still full, and put it on the bench beside the slopped one.

"Stay," she said. "It's pouring. You can get out to Linden Rise from here as easy as from yours."

And Isabel wanted to. That was the worst of it. She was working it out before she could stop herself: the 14 from Weir Street, the change at the station, the train up the line. It worked from here. It worked better from here. She could have the end of the sofa and the strip of amber on the floor, and Natalie making toast in the morning with the wrong hand, like Thursday. On Thursday she would have stayed without being asked.

But Natalie had asked after. After Salter's Lane, and after the letter that was none of Isabel's business. As if the one made up for the other.

"No," she said. "I'll go home."

She zipped her jacket, one-handed, which took two goes. "Five, Tuesday. Salter's Lane. From the top, not the bottom. Feel the iron first. I'll do it."

"Iz."

"I said I'll do it."

Natalie stood by the bench with her bandaged hand at her side. Isabel went past the coat on its hook to the door. Her sleeve brushed the hem of it, and the paper in the inside pocket made its small sound, and she opened the door and went down into the rain, and stood a minute at the bottom of the stairs, getting wet, before she made herself walk.

She pressed her scalded right palm against the iron bar until the wound opened, and said she knew where Hyejin had been. She paid for her beans by phone, though Hyejin wanted nothing, and worked the grate from the kerb with her staff.

After eight that night Isabel went to Natalie's flat on Weir Street. Natalie's right hand was in new gauze. Isabel told her about the cellar. Natalie said Isabel would work the Salter's Lane grate alone on Tuesday at five. Isabel asked what was in the inside pocket of Natalie's coat. Natalie said it was a letter from home. Isabel left into the rain.
