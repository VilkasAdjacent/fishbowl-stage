Day 4: The Stall Woman Gave a Girl a Tomato and Asked Nothing

Saturday 7 July 2046, morning

Keep off the drains on Linnet Lane. Ask before you go down.Handwritten note pinned inside the lobby of Kell's Lane block, 4 Kell's Lane

Amara Nwosu came off the tram at the top of Lockyer Street a step behind Mum, with the satchel strap across her chest and the meter inside it, and the dressing on her wrist under two sleeves. It was Saturday. Mum worked for Second Growth, which wanted to build its hall on the edge of the Tangle, and she was walking the site once more before Tuesday's consultation. She had brought Amara rather than leave her at home on Rowan Crescent with an arm she didn't trust. Her lanyard was on, though nobody was paying her today.

Lockyer Street ran down toward the canal between old warehouses with flats in them. Halfway down, a white van was parked half on the kerb with the Second Growth leaf on its side. As Amara passed it the satchel swung hard against her hip, out and back, twice, though she hadn't changed her step. She put her hand flat on the flap. The signal behind her teeth stayed quiet the whole length of the van, and quiet after it.

At the bottom the wall began, long and brick, with trees standing up over it much too big for a park. There was a flyer on every pole, SAVE THE TANGLE over a drawn vine. By the gate a stall had its shutter up and its trestles out, and in the window behind the tomatoes sat a council notice about the hall, curling at the corners. A woman with greying hair tied back in green garden twine was weighing tomatoes for an old man in a cap. Amara had bought tomatoes from this stall last summer, a whole bag, while Mum walked the site with her plans.

What Amara wanted was ten minutes along that wall with the box out and nobody asking what it was.

"Can I walk to the end and back?" she asked. "You'd see me the whole way. It's straight."

"To the gate," Mum said. "Where I can see you without turning round."

"The gate's right there."

"Then it won't take you long."

Mum went up to the stall with her meeting face on, the friendly one. "Morning. You'll be Hyejin Park. Your name's on every pole from the tram."

Amara stopped with her hand on the satchel buckle. Hyejin. The three messages, the night she'd posted: what it costs, how girls die, that nobody is coming. She'd read each one twice and answered none of them, and here was the woman who'd sent them, with dirt in the creases of her hands, weighing tomatoes.

"And you're the hall," Hyejin said.

"I'm Chioma Nwosu. I'm running Tuesday evening. I wanted to see it in daylight before I stand up in front of people who love it."

"Then tell me in daylight. Why here? There's the old gasworks. There's half of Corrigan standing empty."

Amara went along to the gate. It was iron, painted green a long time ago, and the trees inside came right up to it. She turned her back to the stall, so her body was between the box and Mum, and took the meter out and held it low against her side.

The needle sat where it always sat now, a hair above zero, where the pin had bent. She tipped the box toward the gate. The needle lifted past the first felt-tip mark, and on, slowly, to the second, and stayed there. She turned it toward the wall. It dropped a little. Back to the gate, and up to the second again, steady, not jumping the way it had at the culvert.

Some of that was the pin. Since the bend the box read high, and she'd only ever seen by how much at rest: a hair. Whether it stayed a hair when the needle climbed, or got bigger the further it went, she had no way to check out here. So two marks was real, or one and a bit was, or less. She couldn't say which.

"The canal water and the grid connection," Mum was saying behind her. "It's all in the brief, and you'll get the whole of it Tuesday, with the maps up."

"I don't want the maps. I want you to say it to me, now, without a room round you."

"I'd rather say it once, properly, with everyone there, than give you half of it at a stall and have half go round as the whole."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the one I've got before Tuesday."

"Have you been in? Past the gate? Have you put your hand in those beds?"

"I have. It's remarkable. I'm not going to stand there Tuesday and pretend it isn't."

"We'll have three hundred names on Tuesday," Hyejin said. "Three hundred, from this street and the next two."

"Good. Bring them all. I mean that."

Amara glanced back. Mum had her notebook open on the end of the trestle and was writing in it, 300, with a line under it. She still hadn't said why here. She'd said Tuesday three different ways, and Hyejin hadn't got her past any of them, and now Mum had Hyejin's number four days early, to plan the room round.

Amara moved a step closer to the bars. The needle went no higher. She wanted one more reading, from the corner where the wall met the gatepost, to see if it was the gate or what was behind it.

"What's she reading?"

Hyejin's voice had changed direction. Amara looked round. Hyejin was looking past Mum's shoulder straight at her, at the box, and then lower, at her left wrist, where the sleeve had ridden up off the edge of the white dressing.

"Oh, it's a thing she built," Mum said. "She points it at everything. We're out at Linden Rise, and she's had it at every drain on the estate."

Linden Rise, out loud, to the one person in the Bay who had written to a girl at Linden Rise and been left without an answer. Hyejin looked at her a second time, longer, the way you'd look at a word you'd only seen written down. If she knew, she could say it to Mum across the tomatoes, or to Isabel, or say it on Tuesday in front of three hundred names.

Amara put the box back in the satchel and buckled it. "It's for physics," she said. "Interference. It's not very good yet."

"Mm," Hyejin said.

She picked a tomato off the top of the pile, the reddest one, and held it out over the trestle. Amara came and took it. It was warm, though the morning wasn't.

"On the house," Hyejin said, to Amara and not to Mum. "Grown just there. You'll be at the back on Tuesday, I expect."

Mum paid for a kilo and said thank you, and took Amara's elbow, the good one, and they walked back up Lockyer Street past the van. Amara held the tomato in her right hand the whole way to the tram, and didn't eat it.

Isabel came back to Kell's Lane at midday with the pesto jar in her jacket and her right hand in her pocket, and found Mrs Vella already at the downpipe in her coat, holding a key on a loop of string.

The static was fainter than yesterday. Isabel had to stand still at the drain grate at the foot of the downpipe with her eyes shut before she found it at all, a bad line behind her ear that cut out for five seconds at a time. Along the foot of the wall the seam she'd opened and lost had closed over again, crooked, the mortar knitting back in a line that wandered off from where her staff had run. Yesterday it would have taken an hour to open. Today she wasn't sure she could find enough of it to start.

"That's the cellar," Mrs Vella said, and held the key up. "The block's. I've had it since the caretaker died in oh-three, and nobody's ever asked for it back."

"Can I borrow it?"

"You can tell me first whether the little ones from number nine are safe."

"They're safe if they keep off the drains."

"That's not safe, that's careful. I'm asking whose job it is. The council's, or somebody's."

Isabel looked at the drain grate. "Somebody's."

"Yours, then." Mrs Vella didn't hand the key over. "In ninety-eight they had this lane up seven weeks. The old drain's brick, under Linnet Lane, from when the lanes were laid. They ran the new pipe from the downpipe under the block and joined it on to the brick one, under the cellar floor, and that joint never sat right. Every winter since, that cellar's smelled. The Grech boy at number four was poorly the whole of that year and nobody could say with what." She held out the key at last. "I'm not asking what you are. I've seen you on that stair at four in the morning for five years and I've not asked. I'm asking you to come up and tell me straight after."

"I'll tell you," Isabel said, and took it in her left hand.

The cellar door was round the side of the block under the fire stair, and the steps went down steep into a smell of wet brick. Mrs Vella stayed at the top with her hand on the rail.

At the bottom there was a bulb that didn't work, a row of meters, three bins and a pram with no wheels. Isabel stood on the concrete and called her staff.

The pale wood came into her hands and the copper ran down both forearms, and the shape settled over her, and she stayed a moment as she was with the staff upright beside her before she put its end to the floor.

Her right hand hadn't closed past halfway since yesterday morning. She'd meant to hold her phone in it for the torch. It wouldn't hold the phone and the staff both, and the phone slid down her fingers on to the concrete, so she left it there face up, throwing a white circle on the ceiling, and worked by what came back off the walls.

The static came up through the floor, or seemed to. The crooked knit was in the floor too, a line where the damp wouldn't sit, and she followed that with the staff end, a hand's width at a time, the way she'd followed the seam along the wall. It ran across the slab from the side nearest the lane toward the back wall, the Linnet Lane side, wandering left of where any pipe ought to go. Twice the static dropped out altogether and she stood in the dark with the staff on the concrete and waited for it, and twice it came back fainter, and she couldn't have said from where.

By the back wall there was an inspection cover set into the floor, cast iron, the size of a tea tray. The knit ran straight to it.

Never walk up on one alone. Natalie had said it on the first night Isabel ever followed her, and Isabel had said it to Amara at the culvert on Thursday, and neither time had either of them said why. Mrs Vella at the top of the stairs didn't count. Natalie was on Weir Street, and if Isabel went and got her, Natalie would come down these steps first, and it would be Natalie's root again, and Natalie's jar.

She put the staff in the crook of her left arm and got her fingers under the edge of the lid. It was heavy. She got it up a hand's width with her left hand and the heel of her right, and looked under.

The brick drain ran across under the lid, and on the joint where the new pipe met it there was a dark stain on the brick, warm, the air over it moving a little. Nothing else. Whatever had sat there had gone.

Then the static came, loud, from behind her: the lane side of the cellar, under the bins, the way she'd walked in. The knit had led her across the floor to the back wall, and the root had been back there by the door the whole time, a metre off its own seam.

It didn't wait for her to turn. It went from loud to far in the time it took her to look round, down and along under the side wall, and halfway it dropped out and didn't come back. Toward Linnet Lane, or back under Kell's Lane, or somewhere else, she couldn't tell.

Her right hand gave. The lid came down into its frame and caught the first two fingers of that hand against the iron rim before she got them out. Under the gauze the knuckles went hot and then wet.

A root with no nerves didn't run before you'd reached it. She had taken every nerve it had on Tuesday night. It had felt her coming across a cellar floor in the dark.

She stood with the staff until she was sure there was nothing to hear. She let the shape go, and the cold came straight through her jacket. Picking her phone up off the floor took her three goes with the left hand, because she wouldn't use the right, and when she went up the steps she held the rail with her elbow.

"Well?" Mrs Vella said.

"I don't know where it is," Isabel said. "It was here and it isn't. Keep them off the drains on Linnet Lane as well."

Mrs Vella looked at her for a while, and at the gauze, which had gone brown at the knuckles. "That's my side."

"I know."

"Then you'd better come in and have a cup of tea and tell me what that means," Mrs Vella said, and held out her hand for the key, and waited.

The rain came back to the old core in the middle of the afternoon, and by four Natalie was out in it, going down to look at the long grate under the tram stop, which flooded first in any rain. The way there took her along the Tangle's wall.

Hyejin Park had the stall half packed in the wet, crates of tomatoes stacked under a tarp, the council notice in the window gone soft and dark at its corners. She was folding a trestle when Natalie came along the footpath, and she spoke before Natalie had decided whether to nod.

"Your Second Growth woman walked the site this morning."

"She's not mine."

"She brought her kid. Fifteen or so, a jacket too big for her, a dressing on her wrist. Had a box with a dial on it she kept pointing at my gate when her mother wasn't looking." Hyejin got the trestle flat and leaned it on the wall. "Linden Rise, the mother said. I wrote to a girl at Linden Rise, three weeks found. Never heard a word back. Your Isabel got to her first."

A box with a needle that went to the stop. Iz had told her all of it under the pole on Thursday night. Natalie stopped at the stall because walking on now would be its own answer.

"I'll take some beans, if you've any left," she said.

Hyejin found half a bag under the tarp and didn't weigh it. "Come in and see the beds. Five minutes. The rain's nothing under the trees."

"I've the tram stop grate to look at before it goes under."

"It goes under every time. It'll go under whether you're stood over it or not."

The canal was up. Natalie could hear it past the end of the wall, louder than this morning, and she knew without looking how high it would be against the stone at the bridge, and where the patch was, under it, and that it would be pulling. Hyejin had come round the end of the stall and was standing by the gate. From there she could see along the towpath to the bridge as well as Natalie could.

Natalie kept her eyes on Hyejin.

"I've walked past this gate twenty-one years," she said. "I'm not going to start in the rain with a wet boot."

"Then don't start. Just come in the once." Hyejin had the twine come loose at the back of her hair, and she didn't fix it. "They'll pave it inside a year, Nat. Then you won't have to walk past it ever again. You'll have that."

"Don't."

The pull came stronger, the way it did when the water rose, low in her, like the slope of a floor. To keep from turning her head toward the bridge Natalie put her right hand on the gate. She had a scald across the base of that palm, over the callus, from the grate in Salter's Lane, wet and soft in the middle. The bar was cold and wet. She held it, and kept her face on Hyejin's, and the soft middle of the scald opened against the iron. She felt it go, and kept holding.

"I don't need you to ask me anything," Hyejin said. "I've never needed that. I was on that bridge. You know I was on that bridge. You could stand on this side of a gate for me, once, and look."

There it was, said, on a wet Saturday at a market stall, with the beans going soft in their bag. Hyejin's mouth shut after it. Her hands, gardener's hands with the dirt in the creases, had gone still on the tarp.

"I know where you were," Natalie said. "I'm going to the tram stop."

She took her hand off the gate. There was copper-brown on the green paint where her palm had been, a smear of it, and the rain started on it at once. Hyejin looked at the bar and then at the hand.

"What's that?"

"Salter's Lane. The frost had it in the iron."

"You want that wrapped."

"I've stuff at home." Natalie put the hand in her coat pocket and found the letter there with her fingers, and moved them off it, and held the beans in the other. "What do I owe you?"

"Nothing."

Natalie put two coins on the trestle anyway. Hyejin left them there in the wet, turned her back, and went on pulling the tarp over the crates, tucking the corners under, one and then the next, harder than the job needed.

When Natalie got to the tram stop, the grate was under four fingers of brown water. She did it from the kerb side, kneeling, with the staff in her left hand.

Isabel came up the stairs on Weir Street a little after eight with the rain still on her jacket. Natalie had the door open before she knocked, and the kettle was already going.

Natalie's boots stood on newspaper by the heater, toes to it, and the narrow strip of amber from the baseboard runner lay across the floorboards between them. Her right hand had new gauze on it, round the palm and between the thumb and finger.

"What happened?"

"Gate," Natalie said.

"What gate?"

"The Tangle's."

Isabel looked at her. "You went in the Tangle."

"No." Natalie filled two mugs left-handed and slopped one, and left it. "Sit down. You've been at the lane."

Isabel didn't sit. She told it standing by the bench, in order, because that was the only way it would come out: Mrs Vella and the key, ninety-eight, the brick drain under Linnet Lane and the joint that never sat right. The knit across the cellar floor, and the cover it ran her to, with nothing under it but a warm stain on the brick. "And then it went. From behind me, by the door. Before I'd got anywhere near it. I took every nerve it had on Tuesday night, and it felt me coming across a floor."

Natalie's face did nothing, which meant something. "Which way?"

"I don't know. I lost it halfway." She held up her hand in its gauze, brown now at the knuckles. "And the lid came down on this."

"You went down there on your own."

"Mrs Vella was at the top of the stairs."

"Leave Kell's Lane, then," Natalie said. "Till I can come with you."

Isabel picked up the mug that wasn't slopped. "Okay."

"I had a girl with a box at the Tangle's wall this morning," Natalie said. "Hyejin told me. Fifteen, a jacket too big, a dressing on her wrist. Her mother's the Second Growth woman running Tuesday."

"Amara?" Isabel put the mug down. "Her mum's Second Growth? She never said. She doesn't say anything. She just turns up knowing it." So that was another thing. Another thing that was going to come out the day after Isabel needed it. "I'm at Linden Rise tomorrow. Ten, at the culvert. She wants me to watch her hold the box to the grate."

"Good."

"Good," Isabel said. "Okay."

Natalie sat down on the arm of the sofa with her mug, under the tall glass, the radio towers blinking red on the ridge behind her in the rain. "Salter's Lane," she said. "If the thaw comes Tuesday, it's five in the morning. It's yours. I won't be there."

"Where will you be?"

"Here. In bed, if I've any sense."

"So I get your grate, on my own, with a hand that doesn't close, and you keep my lane."

"The lane's got a root that can feel you, in a drain you can't see into. Salter's Lane is a grate you've watched me do fifty times."

"And the Friday after? Is the chapel yard mine too? The tram stop?"

Natalie drank some of her tea.

The coat was on its hook by the door, olive and long, and the inside pocket sat flat against the lining where Isabel couldn't see into it. All week Natalie's hand had gone into that pocket and come out empty.

"What's in your pocket?" Isabel asked. "You've had your hand on it since Wednesday."

"A letter."

"From who?"

"Home."

"Port Ellery." Isabel waited, and nothing else came. "Is that what Tuesday is? You asked me why Tuesday yesterday like it was on a card, and it's not about the thaw, is it. Nobody plans a grate four days out."

"Tuesday's Salter's Lane. If it thaws."

"That's not what I asked."

"It's what I can tell you."

"Can, or will?" It came out too loud for the flat. Natalie looked at her and didn't answer that either, and Isabel heard herself go on, which she shouldn't have. "My name's never gone in the book. Not once, in five years. Every jar's gone up that step in your hand. And now I'm doing your round."

Natalie looked at her. It went on a moment too long, longer than it took to hear what Isabel had said. Then she put her mug down on the floor by her foot.

"Have you still got the jar clean?"

Isabel laughed, once, not much. "It's clean. It's in my jacket. There's nothing in it."

"Keep it that way till there is."

The rain went sideways across the glass. Natalie got up and took Isabel's mug, which was still full, and put it on the bench beside the slopped one.

"Stay," Natalie said. "It's pouring. You can get out to Linden Rise from here as easy as from yours."

It was the one thing all night that sounded like Thursday. It came after Salter's Lane and the letter, and that was the order she heard it in.

"Hoshi's not fed," she said.

She zipped her jacket, one-handed, which took two goes. "Five, Tuesday. Salter's Lane. From the top, not the bottom. Feel the iron first. I'll do it."

"Iz."

"I said I'll do it."

Natalie stood with her bandaged hand by her side and didn't reach for anything. Isabel went past the coat on its hook to the door. Her sleeve brushed the hem of it, and the paper in the inside pocket made its small sound, and she opened the door and went down into the rain.

Her mother had brought her because of the injured wrist. Amara carried a meter in her satchel. She pressed her scalded right palm against the iron bar until the wound opened, and said she knew where Hyejin had been. After eight that night Isabel went to Natalie's flat on Weir Street. Natalie's right hand was in new gauze. Isabel told her about the cellar. Natalie said Isabel would work the Salter's Lane grate alone on Tuesday at five. Isabel asked what was in the inside pocket of Natalie's coat. Natalie said it was a letter from home. Isabel left into the rain.
