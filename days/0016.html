Day 16: The Girl Who Slipped on Ice Too Thin to Wet a Cuff

Friday 13 July 2046, morning

Black ice reported at the main gates. Pupils please use the side entrance.a Linden Rise School text to parents

Hyejin Park was on the eighth of the old woman's eleven beds when the gate went. The beds ran in a strip between the back wall and the lane, each the length of a door, and she had been turning them since the end of summer for forty a week and lunch on the step. Hyejin was forty-one, stopped at nineteen for eleven years and running again since, and her knees had started to tell her about frost. She did not mind that. A knee that ached was a knee that was going somewhere.

She wanted the last three dug before the ground locked for good, and she wanted the back door to stay shut while she did it. Yesterday it had opened. The old woman had come out in her dressing gown with the tablet and asked, kindly, once. Once could stay on the step. If the door opened again it would be twice, and twice was a habit, and a habit was a thing you had to answer.

So when the gate went she did not look up at the house. She looked at the lane.

It was the old woman's daughter, who drove over from the ridge on Fridays with the shopping and called Hyejin the garden lady to her face. She had parked down by the tram stop and walked up. She had no shopping. She had a sheet of stickers in one gloved hand, the backing curled from the cold, and she stopped at the letterbox and peeled one off with her thumbnail.

Hyejin knew the sticker. A blue square, and on it in white the one word, CONTINUITY, and under that, smaller, This household is in good hands. She had seen it on the corner house with the cherry tree, and on the green door by the chemist, and on three or four letterboxes along the tram route. A house got the sticker, and the old person in it stopped coming to the window, and a few weeks later there was a board up with an agent's name on it. Signed over, people said. She had never asked to what. She had a fair idea, and the idea had Catherine's face on a screen at the Tangle fence, talking to whoever stood in front of it.

The daughter pressed the sticker to the letterbox flap and smoothed it out from the middle with her thumb, the way you do so no bubbles get under. It was done before Hyejin had a word ready. She was not sure what the word would have been.

"Morning," the daughter said. "God, it's cold. Mum says you're nearly finished."

"Eight," Hyejin said. "Three to go."

"Right. About that." The daughter came along the wall with the sheet of stickers held out from her coat so the backing would not catch. She smiled. She had a nice smile, her mother's. "You can leave them, honestly. They won't be needed after the 24th. The plot's going with the house."

"The house is going."

"It's gone, more or less. A couple from the ridge. They've got a little boy, they'll want it all down to lawn." She looked at the bed Hyejin was on, the turned half black and the unturned half white, and said it the way you would tell someone which day the bins went out. "Seems a shame to have you break your back for grass."

Hyejin leaned on the spade. Through the kitchen window there was the amber of the overhead light and a shape at the table that did not move. She did not look at it straight.

"She said she asked you something yesterday," the daughter said. "About that video. I'm sorry. She gets stuck on things now. She won't be bothering you."

There it was. The question had come round again after all, the long way, in someone else's mouth, and it had come round only to be put away. Nobody was going to ask it from that step now. The woman who had asked it would be on a list somewhere by the 24th, with a sticker on her letterbox saying she was in good hands. Hyejin had spent a whole day with the spade going so as not to hear that question, and now it would never be asked again, and she could not see what she had won.

"I'll finish them," she said.

The daughter tilted her head.

"Three beds. Two days if the ground gives. I won't charge you." She heard it come out and thought, Christ, listen to you. She had not asked anyone for anything in eleven years. Not Elspeth, not the council, not the girl under the hatch lid, who had asked her. And here she was asking to dig a stranger's lawn for free.

"That's really kind," the daughter said. "But there's no point, is there? They'll only grass it over. I'll pay you to the end of the month. Mum wanted that."

Hyejin did not answer. The daughter waited, and then she smiled again and went up the path to the back door with her stickers, and the door opened and the warm came out and the door shut.

The bed lay at her feet half done. She could finish it. Her hands knew how. She could turn the last of the eighth and start on the ninth, and every spadeful would be laid down for a boy she would never meet to run about on, and for the couple from the ridge to look out at, and they would never know the soil had been opened at all. She would not give them one more. Not one.

She pulled the spade out of the frozen mulch. A clod came up with it and she knocked it off on her boot. Gloves into the bag, trowel into the bag, the twine and the knife after them. She put the spade in the barrow because that was where the spade went at the end of a day, and she wheeled the barrow down to the wall by the tap and stood it on its nose against the bricks, and she walked out through the gate.

The sticker on the letterbox was flat and bright and had no bubbles under it. She went past it.

She was at the tram stop before she knew the spade was still in the barrow. It was hers. She had brought it from the Tangle, the one with the handle she had taped where it split the winter after she started running again. Eleven years in her hands. Going back for it meant the gate, and the path, and the amber window with the shape at the table, and the daughter coming out to say it was no trouble at all.

She stayed at the stop.

Behind her, between the wall and the lane, the beds lay half-dug, and the frost was already settling on the earth she had opened, white over black, as if no one had touched it.

Amara Nwosu stood at the edge of the drain lake with her right sleeve dark to the shoulder, and Elspeth Hale came down the path with the register under her arm.

Elspeth Hale was one hundred and twenty, stopped at seventeen. Her coat had a belt and a collar that had gone out of fashion a long time ago, and she crossed the flooded margin of the old playing field as though it were a room she knew. The goalposts stood out of the water with the paint flaking off them. Past the posts the ring road ran, and under the ring road the culvert, and the hum out of the culvert sat in Amara's back teeth.

She wanted the old woman gone. If Elspeth Hale got a proper look at the sleeve there would be a hand on her shoulder walking her up the lane, or a call to the house, or a page in the register with her name at the top of it, and the lake would go on without her.

"You're wet," Elspeth said.

"I fell."

"Where?"

"By the gates." Amara kept it flat. "Black ice."

Elspeth looked at the sleeve. Then she looked at the water, and then at her own hand, which she held up and turned over, watching it the way you watch a borrowed tool.

"The ice by the gates is a skim," she said. "It would not wet a cuff."

"I went down like a bin bag."

"You went down on ice," Elspeth said, "and got up wet to the shoulder."

"It was a big fall."

"Let me see the arm. I have looked at worse."

She moved. One step, unhurried, and the hand coming out with the fingers open. She was going to take the sleeve and turn it back and see the arm, and the arm had a line on it from the wrist past the crook of the elbow with copper gone dry along the edge of it, and one look was all it would take.

"Stay back."

It came out of Amara before she had it ready, and it came out hard — harder than she had ever said anything to an adult. Her ears rang.

The hand came back. Elspeth's fingers closed on nothing, and she watched them do it, as if they belonged to somebody else and had made a small mistake.

"Very well, Amara Nwosu," she said.

She turned and went up the path between the half-drowned bins, her feet finding the dry places without her looking down, and she did not turn round at the top.

The hum went on under the ring road. It was bigger than the ones she had cut, and it had been bigger every day, and it would be there tomorrow whether she stood at the water or not. She had got what she asked for. Nobody had seen the arm. Nobody was going to stand beside her at the culvert either, and there was nobody left on the path to ask.

The Bay Sightings board was still open on her, the stranger's reply at the top and her own question under it and no answer yet. She had put it to the handle — what did you see at the lake — and sent it before she could take it back. The one person who had walked the footpath and looked at the water was a stranger with no face. The one person who had come down to the water had gone up the lane with the register shut under her arm. Amara had watched the pen go down the page and had not asked what it said, and there was no way to ask now that would not sound like she wanted to know.

She stood at the edge and let the hum come, and did not move.

Elspeth Hale did not go home. She carried the register out of Linden Rise and up into the north lanes, where Isabel had left a cut half-done against the brickwork. Four days earlier Elspeth had kept that blight out of the register, and she had told Hyejin Park the lanes were clear. She would not put the entry right until she had seen the blight herself. The frost held on every wall she passed, and nothing thawed.

Chronicle

The next morning in the eastern wards, Hyejin Park was digging the old woman's eighth bed when the old woman's daughter came up the lane. She pressed a blue Continuity sticker onto the letterbox and told Hyejin, pleasantly, that the beds wouldn't be needed after the 24th. The plot would be sold with the house.

The sticker was smoothed flat before Hyejin had a word ready.

She put the spade in the barrow and the barrow against the wall, and walked out through the gate past the sticker, so as not to turn one more spadeful for whoever bought the house. Behind her the beds lay half-dug, the open earth rimed white. She did not go back for them.

At the Linden Rise drain lake, Amara Nwosu stood at the water's edge with her sleeve soaked to the shoulder. Elspeth Hale asked what had happened.

"Black ice by the gates. Went down like a bin bag."

The ice by the gates was a skim that could not wet a cuff. Elspeth reached for the sleeve.

"Stay back."

The hand came back. It had worked. Nobody would see how far the line had opened now. Nobody would stand at the culvert with her either.

"Very well, Amara Nwosu." Elspeth left her at the lake and walked up to the north lanes with the register, because the entry would not be right until she had seen that blight herself.
