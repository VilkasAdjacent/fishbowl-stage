Day 14: The Girl Who Turned From the Camera a Second Too Late

Wednesday 11 July 2046, morning

One life. Live it where people can see it.the sign-off on the One Life channels

Before nine the kitchen window went blue.

She saw it the way she saw the bus at the end of the lane: without turning her head, from the corner of an eye, with no interest. The tablet stood propped against the tap. The light off it laid a bar on the ceiling, and the ceiling was what she had instead of a look.

Frost had gone into the mulch in the night. The spade rang on the first stroke and on every stroke after, one flat note with no give in it, and after a while the note was the work.

She took the second bed off the wall, where the frost had lifted the mat of it and let the cold in under. Eleven beds, north to south, between the wall and the lane. The old woman had kept them before her knees went. Now she kept the window.

The certificate was pinned in the shed with a nail. The Thirty-Third: Hoary Cress, cleared in the autumn. She had dug two roots of it since and not told the old woman. Two roots was nothing yet. She turned them into the mulch and let the cold take them.

Forty-one, stopped at nineteen for eleven years, running again since. The hands said thirty. There was dirt in the creases of them and they did what she asked.

At ten past nine the old woman came to the window with the tablet up in both hands and put it to the glass. Then she knocked on the glass with one knuckle, twice, the way you knock on a door. Her hands were the colour of tea and the knuckles were big, and she held the tablet the way you hold a plate with something on it.

Hyejin set the spade point-down in the ground and looked at the black she had turned. Crumbs of frost in it and the soft dark under. She had seen the cut on her own phone before she came out, standing in the dark of the shed with the sound down, and it ran short. Catherine's avatar kept its place over the brass, and her own face had been turning from the lens, and had turned a second late.

Lab staff still won't say what happened in 2035. That was the line. She was lab staff, and she still didn't. She took up the spade again.

The window came open a hand's width at the top, where it always stuck, and the cold came in on the old woman and the voice came out.

"Hyejin."

"Ma'am."

The old woman waited at the glass. Hyejin knocked the frost off the blade with the flat of her hand and set it back in the ground, and the waiting went on, and there was nothing she had to put into it.

The window came down. The catch turned. The tablet stayed up on the glass a while longer with the picture going, and after that it came down as well, and the bar of blue left the ceiling.

A new Yes placard, before the vote. The one in the shed had gone grey at the corners and the tape had dried out. She could walk down to the office for one after lunch, if she went at all.

She broke the frost on the third bed and the fourth. The blade rang the same note it had rung at eight, and the sun came over the wall at noon and the frost went soft on top and stayed in underneath. She had broken seven of the eleven and had not looked up once.

At Linden Rise, Amara Nwosu sat on the end of her bed and opened the Bay Sightings thread on her phone. Her fingers shook, and the first tap missed. The reply from the unknown handle still sat at the top of the board, blue under the banner, where it had sat all night. Nobody had taken it down. She read it from the first word to the last.

The water had gone down in the night.

Elspeth Hale stood at the top of the low crossing with the register held flat against her coat and looked at it. Yesterday it had come over the causeway brown and quick and taken her to the knee before she had gone ten steps. Now it lay a hand's depth over the concrete and no more, and the frost had set the mud on either side into ridges that took the print of a boot and kept it. A line of straw and plastic lay along the bank a yard above the water, where the flood had stood and left its edge.

She went down onto the causeway. The kerb on the upstream side stood clear of the water by an inch, white with rime, and she walked it heel to toe as she had walked it in other winters, in other boots. The register stayed in the crook of her arm because her fingers would not close on it. Halfway over, the culvert spoke under her, a hollow knock of something lodged and turning, and she did not look down. On the far side the frost held, and she stepped off onto it dry.

A hundred and twenty, stopped at seventeen. Her jacket was a waxed one with a cord collar, bought new in a year when everyone had one, and the wellingtons had a buckle at the calf that nobody made any more.

She stood on the far bank and put the backs of her fingers to her mouth. Her lips felt them. The fingers did not feel her lips. The pen was in the inside pocket, against her shirt, where it had been since the flood, and she had not taken it out to try it.

By ten the drains behind her were running loud. She heard them all the way up the lane, a steady pour from every grating, and she did not turn round to see.

The footpath to Linden Rise went off the lane between a wall and a run of beds. Someone had cut new posts for the stile and not yet tarred them. Elspeth Hale went over it slowly, register first, set down on the top bar and taken up again on the far side, and came along the beds with the wall on her left.

Hyejin Park was in the eighth bed. She had her sleeves pushed up and her coat off and hung on the wall, and the spade went into the frozen mulch and rang, and came out, and went in again. There was black soil to the shins of her trousers. The seven beds behind her were turned and dark, and the four ahead of her were still white.

At the house end of the beds a window stood shut. A tablet lay on the sill inside it, face down.

"Good morning, Hyejin Park."

The spade went in.

"The crossing's down," Elspeth Hale said. "I came over on the kerb."

"Miss Hale."

That was all. Hyejin Park put her boot to the shoulder of the blade and leaned on it, and the frost broke with a sound like a plate, and she turned the clod over and struck it twice with the edge. She did not look up. Her hair was tied back with a bit of green garden twine, and there was a cut of light across the back of her neck where the sun had come over the wall.

Elspeth Hale waited the length of three more strokes. Then she went on along the path, and the spade rang behind her at the same pace as before, neither faster nor slower.

The path dropped past the last of the beds and through a gap where a fence had been, and Linden Rise was below her.

It had been a playing field. The goalposts were still there at the near end, white paint flaking off the steel, the crossbar standing a foot clear of the water. Beyond them the lake lay flat and grey to the backs of the houses, and on its far side a row of bins stood on a pavement that went down into it and did not come up. A child's bicycle lay on its side at the margin with one wheel out and the spokes furred with ice.

She had put a stake in at Easter. A length of dowel, painted white at the top, driven in at the edge with the heel of her boot. She found it by the white. It stood a long stride out from the bank now, with water to its middle and a collar of ice round it, and the ground where she had stood to drive it in was under the lake as well.

Along the margin the weed came up through the old cinder of the running track. It was low, and finely cut at the leaf, and green the way things are green that grow on ash. It ran in a band between the water and the dry ground as if somebody had laid it there with a rule. Where she stood, it had come up through the frost round the toes of her boots.

There was a bench by the gap with a brass plate on it for someone who had loved this field. Elspeth Hale sat on the end of it and laid the register across her knees. The cover was cold enough to feel through the cloth of her skirt. She opened it with the heel of her hand, and the pages went over stiffly, and came to rest at the last entry in her own small upright hand, and the ruled line under it with nothing on it.

Up in one of the houses that backed onto the water, a curtain was open and a light was on. Somebody's room. She could see the end of a bed and nothing else.

She took the pen out of her shirt. She had to look at her hand to see that she had it, and look again to see that the cap was off. She laid the nib on the line and held it there, and watched it, because watching was the only way she had of knowing it was touching the page.

Chronicle

A day on, ice had skinned the puddles in the eastern wards.

Before nine, Dev Okafor's cut of the Tangle unveiling went up on the eastern wards feed and the One Life channels. It showed Hyejin Park turning from his camera, a second too late, beside Catherine Lau's avatar and the brass plaque. Across the bottom ran the caption: Lab staff still won't say what happened in 2035.

The old woman whose beds Hyejin worked had it open on her tablet at the kitchen window, the screen blue against the glass.

Hyejin kept her head down and spaded the frozen mulch. The blade rang at every stroke.

The old woman had her face and Dev's words, and so did every girl in the wards with a phone. The thing she had never said was out there to be read. The spade was loud. If she did not look up, there was nothing on that tablet anybody could ask her about. So she did not look up.

At Linden Rise, Amara Nwosu opened the Bay Sightings thread with shaking fingers. The reply from the unknown handle still sat at the top of the board. Nobody had taken it down.

Elspeth Hale tried the low crossing again, alone. The meltwater had dropped in the night, and the frost had firmed the edges. By ten the gutters ran loud behind her, and she was across. She came up the footpath to Linden Rise with the register under her arm.
