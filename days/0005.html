Day 5: The Woman Who Wrote Her Round on an Envelope in the Rain

Sunday 8 July 2046, night

Council drains: do not enter standing water. Report any cover that has lifted.Civic Works notice, cable-tied to the towpath railing at Lockyer Street

Isabel came off the bus at the Linden Rise Centre at five to ten, after the tram, the train up the Kilbride line to Breen's Crossing and the bus round the ring road. Overnight the first two fingers of her right hand had come up purple under the gauze, across the two scalds already there, and they wouldn't bend at all now. She'd paid for every leg with her phone in her left hand, and dropped it once on the train.

She'd fed Hoshi at seven with the tin pinned under her elbow. The cat ate half and went back to the corner behind the desk, and when Isabel put her palm to the plaster there, the wall was colder than either side of it.

The drain lake was still over the path. The reeds at the culvert grate had gone black at the root, and the grey water lay flat against the concrete lip of the pipe under the ring road. Amara was waiting well back from it, up by the bench where the path went under, with the box held in both hands against her school jacket.

So she'd kept that much.

The ground was wrong. In the north lanes Isabel could have walked with her eyes shut and felt which way every lane fell. Out here it was thin under her trainers, new, like boards laid last week over a drop, and her feet kept testing before they trusted it. It wasn't hers, and it didn't feel as if anyone had walked it long enough for it to be anybody's.

She wanted the next hour in the right order: Amara behind her, saying before she moved, and Isabel looking like someone who'd done this before. And she didn't want to think about the old core, where Natalie was getting on with a Sunday that didn't have her in it.

"You waited," Isabel said.

"You said to. I haven't been nearer than this bench since Thursday." Amara held up the box. "The joint's done properly now. The pin's bent, so it reads high, about a hair. I measured it on the kitchen table. I want you to watch it at the grate. That's the whole thing."

"Okay." Isabel had worked out on the train what she'd say first. "Two things before anything. If it's grown nerves, you take the nerves first. And you never walk up on one alone."

"Why the nerves first?"

"So it can't feel you coming and run."

"Okay." Amara was looking past her at the grate. "And alone means without another girl?"

"It means you tell me before you move."

"That's three."

"That one's mine. The other two are Natalie's. She's been doing this twenty-nine years."

"Nerves first, so it can't run. Never walk up alone. Tell you before I move." Amara said them back in order, word for word. "So if I've told you first, is it okay to go?"

That wasn't the rule. It was the rule with a door in it, and Amara was already looking through the door at the grate. Isabel opened her mouth to say why it was wrong, and the why wasn't there. She'd been handed the rule and never the reason, and all she had in its place was a cellar floor and a noise under some bins, which wasn't a thing you could hand anyone.

"The hair," she said instead. "Where did you measure it?"

"The kitchen."

"So measure it here. This isn't your kitchen."

She watched Amara get it, all of it, in one go. Amara held the box flat on the bench, where the static in Isabel's head thinned to nothing, and waited. The needle sat higher than a hair, nearly its own width off the stop. Amara uncapped a felt-tip with her teeth, drew a new line on the dial, small and exact, and capped it.

"That's zero. Here." She looked at Isabel. "Can I take it up to the grate now? You're here. You'll be watching."

"Okay. Slowly, and stay behind-"

"I'm going now," Amara said. "I'm telling you."

She'd told her, so by her own count she'd kept all three, and she was off the path and into the lake, the water over her boots and up her shins in four steps, the box held out in front of her toward the grate.

"Amara-"

She didn't stop. She had asked, and said it, and been told okay.

Isabel went in after her. The drain lake filled her trainers, cold enough to stop her breath, and came up to the middle of her shins, and she was behind. She'd come an hour and a half to be in front, and she was wading after a fifteen-year-old with her one good hand out for balance.

The static rose as she closed on the culvert, a bad line climbing into a whine, all of it from behind the bars. Amara stopped an arm's length from the grate and held the box to it, and turned her head just enough to be sure Isabel could see the dial.

The needle came off her new line. It went past the first felt-tip mark and past the second, slowed, and sat a hair under the third. It didn't jump. Amara turned the box a little left and it dipped, and back again, and it came up to the same place and stayed.

"Is that real?" Amara said, without taking her eyes off it. "Tell me if it's real."

Isabel listened to the static behind her ear: loud, close, steady, nothing dropping out. If she'd had to put a mark on it herself she'd have put it about there.

"Yeah," she said. "That's real."

Amara breathed out, and something pale came out between the bars. It was a thread, finger-length and then longer, sliding along the top of the water and up onto the lid of the box toward her hands.

Isabel had Amara's hood in her left fist before she'd decided anything, and pulled. Amara came back off her feet against her. The box went out of her hands, hit the water under the grate dial-down, and sank. The thread lay along the surface where it had gone in, and drew back slowly into the dark.

They stood in the lake. Under the brown water at the foot of the grate there was a corner of black plastic, and then there wasn't.

"That was the only one," Amara said.

Isabel let go of the hood. Getting the box back meant reaching under that thread, and she wasn't sending a kid's arm in there, or her own hand, which couldn't have closed on it anyway. Amara had the reading, and nothing left to show it on, because Isabel had taken it out of her hands without asking.

"I saw it," she said. "Under the third mark. I saw it."

Amara Nwosu stood in the drain lake at the culvert with her hands empty and the reading in her head: just short of the third mark, from a zero she'd drawn here, on this bank. Nobody had written it down. The dial with the line on it was under the grate.

Isabel's right hand had been inside her jacket the whole morning, and the two fingers that showed above the zip had gone purple.

"I'll call mine," Amara said. "You can't hold one. You talk me through it?"

Isabel looked at the grate, and back. "Yeah. Okay. Call it."

The staff came into her hands, pale green and smelling of sap. The copper ran down both her forearms like the inside of a two-pence piece held to a lamp, and the shape came down over her, and she was looking at the top of Isabel's head. She liked that more than she meant to.

"Nerves first," Isabel said behind her. "Leave the heart. It's under the road. You won't reach it from here."

"I'll walk the bank. If you walk slowly they come out after you."

"Who told you that?"

"It's what they do."

She went along the edge where the lip of the culvert ran under the water, a step and a wait and a step. Her feet knew where the bottom shelved. She tried to keep them from showing how well.

The signal climbed behind her teeth. The first thread slid from the grate and along the water after her, the way the other had come after the box. Then a second.

"That one," Isabel said. "Pin it where it comes off the pipe. Then lean."

Amara pinned it at the base and leaned, and the copper went out of her arms, down the green wood and into it, and it went grey and broke up on the water. Then the second. A third slid out low, from under the lip, and lay along the surface toward her ankle, and she took it there.

Then the water moved round Isabel's shins. She had come up on the culvert side, a full step closer to the bars than Amara was, near enough to touch them.

Nothing turned toward her. The fourth thread came out and went past Isabel's knees without a flicker, and came on toward Amara.

"It's not watching me," Isabel said. "I'm closer. It doesn't- it doesn't care where I am."

Amara took the fourth. She stepped back along the bank to see, and the fifth came with her, bending round Isabel the way the lake bent round the post of the path sign.

"You haven't got your staff," Amara said.

Isabel stood very still in the water, looking at the thread and not at her. "Nobody ever said that. Nerves first. Never walk up alone. Nobody said anything about a staff."

It wasn't on the forums either. Amara had read all of them. She put it away where she put things, once, and kept it.

There were six. She took the sixth at the bars, and the signal dropped from a whine to a low hum somewhere under the ring road, steady, with nothing in it reaching back. She stood with the staff across her hands and waited for Isabel to say it.

"Have you done this before?" Isabel said. "Straight. Your feet knew where the bottom went before you put them down."

If she said yes, Isabel would ask where, and when, and what had happened after. Then Amara would be the girl who'd got a lesson by leaving things out, standing in front of the only person who had answered her.

"I've read about nerves," she said. "A lot. Everyone says walk slow." It came out higher than she wanted.

"Nobody on there said it doesn't care about me without a staff. You didn't know that one. But you knew the bottom."

"It's my estate. I've been round this lake since I was five." She said it to the water.

That was even true. Isabel looked at her a while, and then at the road, where the hum was.

"If you've stood at one before," Isabel said, "and it isn't dead, it's still somewhere. That's somebody's drain. I'm not asking to catch you out."

Amara watched the grey bits of thread turn on the water. Anything she said now would be the start of it, so she held the staff and said nothing at all.

"Okay," Isabel said. "Then we're not doing the heart today."

"Okay."

Under the road the heart had nothing left to feel with. Ten minutes, maybe, with the staff along the pipe. Amara held the green wood across her hands and let that go where the reading had gone.

"You didn't need me for any of that, anyway," Isabel said, and turned and waded for the path.

That wasn't that was right. It was about as far from it as you could get and still be talking about the same thing.

On the bank Amara let the shape go. The staff went out of her hands and the ground came up closer, and she was fifteen again, in a school jacket a size too big, with her eyes level with Isabel's chin.

"You were taller than me," Isabel said. "A minute ago."

"I'm still growing," Amara said. "I've got about three years yet."

Isabel opened her mouth and shut it, and looked at the Centre instead. "There's a bus at twelve-forty. If I miss it it's the half-two."

She went up the path squelching. Amara found the felt-tip in her jacket and, below the dressing on her wrist, wrote just under 3 on the back of her hand, small and exact.

Natalie came into Kell's Lane a little after one, with the sky going dark over the river behind the roofs, to look at the cut she had told Iz to leave alone.

The itch was faint. She stood at the downpipe and had to wait for it, and when it came it came from further along than it should have. The seam Iz had opened on Friday had knitted most of the way over. The mortar had closed in a line that wandered off the true one, fainter than last week, and it had crept along the foot of the wall toward the fire stair.

She could do it now: kneel under the stair, call her staff, find the root wherever it had got to and lean until it went to ash. An hour, perhaps. She'd have a jar in her coat by three, and it would go up the step on Garnet Street in her hand, like every one before it. Iz had stood in her kitchen last night and said as much, too loud. Natalie wanted to watch her cut one start to finish with nobody under the stair, and she wouldn't get that by doing this one.

She took the stub of chalk from her inside pocket, the one she kept for marking grates for council men who never came, and set about finding what was left of the line, so Iz would have it.

Her right palm had torn open on the Tangle gate yesterday, and the gauze on it was fresh this morning. She called her staff and took it left-handed, and laid the right along it to steer.

Mrs Vella's back door opened. She came out onto her step with the thermos and two mugs.

"You were here Friday," she said. "On your knees under my stair."

"I was."

"Is it you that's coming back for my drains, or her?"

"Keep the little ones off the stair and the drains," Natalie said.

"That's not what I asked. I've been told keep them off three times now, by her. Who's coming?"

"It'll be seen to."

"By who? The council's a recording." Mrs Vella unscrewed the thermos. "I'm seventy-three. I'll not stand out here and be told seen to."

Natalie leaned on the staff a hand's width further along the wall and felt the itch go under her, faint, crooked, there. She had been answering the small question, and the woman knew it.

"Isabel," she said. "From the second floor. She's coming back for it. If anything changes, tell her. Not me."

"Isabel." Mrs Vella poured. "And you'll have this before you go anywhere."

There was no saying no to the mug. Natalie stood with the mug and the staff in the crook of her arm, and drank it too hot, and the light went another shade toward evening over the river while she did. Mrs Vella watched her drink every mouthful.

She put the mug on the step and took up the wall again. From the downpipe she had it for a metre, perhaps less, and chalked that much as she went, left-handed, a careful line along the cobbles where the itch sat. At the downpipe end she made the small tick she put on every grate she'd done.

Then it went out from under the staff. She leaned harder with the right hand, and the wood slid in the palm, and the gauze went wet through at the base of the thumb. Nothing came back. She went on chalking anyway, along the crooked run of the mortar where it looked to her as if the seam had gone, toward the first iron post of the fire stair. That was the shape of the wall, not the feel of it, and she knew the difference.

Under the stair there was nothing at all. She leaned, and leaned again, and her fingers came off the staff. The first drops came in off the river and spotted the chalk.

She looked back along what she'd drawn. A metre she knew, and three she had put down by eye, in the same careful hand, and no way now of telling Iz which was which. She chalked a short line past the post, where the mortar went on looking crooked, and after it she drew a question mark. She had marked grates in this city for twenty-nine years and never once put a question mark on one.

She let her staff go. Her right hand would not close now past halfway. She set the stub of chalk on the bottom step of the fire stair, under the overhang where the rain couldn't reach it, and went out of the lane.

Isabel came down Kell's Lane at half past four, with the rain in off the river and setting in, her trainers still full of drain lake from Linden Rise and Mrs Vella's key in her left fist. She had come for the key and the story, because she'd said she would. That was all she'd come for.

There was chalk on the cobbles under the fire stair.

It ran along the bottom of the wall from the downpipe, a careful line, with a small tick at the downpipe end. She'd seen that tick on grates for five years, on Natalie's round, every time. Past the first post of the stair the line went on a little way and stopped, and after it there was a question mark. The rain had got in under the edge of the stair and was running the chalk white into the gaps between the stones. On the bottom step, under the overhang, sat the stub it had been drawn with, dry.

Today. On a Sunday, the day after Natalie had told her to keep away from this lane until they could come together.

Mrs Vella had the door open before she knocked.

"Your friend was here," she said. "Coat to her ankles. She drew that, and made a mess of her hand doing it, and stood in my lane drinking my tea. She says it's you coming back for my drains. Not her. I'm to tell you, not her."

Isabel held out the key. Mrs Vella took it and didn't step back to let her in.

"Now. Under the block. You promised."

"It's the old joint," Isabel said. "Like you said. Where they ran the pipe on in ninety-eight."

"I know where the joint is. What's in it?"

"Nothing, now. It was there and it moved off."

"Don't you do that." Mrs Vella's hand closed on the key. "I've had forty years of men in yellow jackets telling me it's damp. You came up my steps yesterday holding the rail with your elbow."

Isabel looked at the wet step between them. She'd said she would tell her.

"Okay. There's a cover by the back wall. I lifted it. The brick's got a mark on it where the pipes join, warm, like something's been sitting there. And there's- something. In the drain. I don't know what you'd call it. When I got across the floor it went round behind me, by the bins, and off along the pipe. Your way, maybe. That's what I found. That's all of it. If the kids keep off the drains it's not going to-"

"Round behind you," Mrs Vella said.

She looked down at her own step, at the stone under her slippers, the way you'd look at a floor you'd just been told was rotten. Then she picked the thermos up off it.

"I'll not sit out over that," she said. "I'm ringing the council's emergency line tonight, and I'll keep ringing until they send someone with a shovel. And this," she held up the key on its string, "doesn't leave my house again. Not for you, love, and not for your friend."

The door shut. The step stood empty, with a wet ring on it where the thermos had been.

Isabel returned to the stair and got down on her knees under it with the rain loud on the iron overhead. She put the fingertips of her left hand to the cobbles beside the chalk, and listened for the static. It came and went under the noise of the rain, a weak signal that cut out for whole seconds, and when it came she couldn't say if it sat under Natalie's line or a hand to the left of it. The chalk was going while she listened. The tick had already run into the grate.

She got her phone out to photograph it, and the rain beaded on the lens, and the first picture was white smears on grey. The second was worse.

She could have called her staff and known in a minute. Her right hand wouldn't close on it, and the moment it came into her hands she'd be working the lane she'd said okay to leaving. She put the phone away.

Natalie didn't leave notes. Natalie showed you on the day, standing next to you, and asked what came next. You wrote things down for somebody when you weren't going to be standing next to them. Salter's Lane, Tuesday, I won't be there. The round asked back over the sink on Friday, every grate in order. And now this, chalked out in the rain with a question mark on the end. It didn't look like someone taking the lane off her. It looked like someone putting it in order before she went.

The last of the line under the stair went soft and ran.

Isabel picked the chalk stub off the step with her left hand. Under the stair, on the bricks of the wall where the rain didn't reach, she drew the line again from memory: downpipe, post, the short bit past it. It came out crooked, because it was her wrong hand, and because she couldn't remember where the question mark had been. She drew one anyway.

The rain came in harder after dark, and by seven the old canal was a hand below the towpath and still coming up the stone. Natalie went out to the long grate under the tram stop, which flooded first in any rain. Her right hand had opened again on the staff in Kell's Lane, and the fingers wouldn't close past halfway now. She'd do the grate from the kerb, left-handed, if she got there before it went under.

Round by Lockyer Street was twenty minutes. The towpath was eight. At the top of the steps a laminated council notice was cable-tied to the railing, warning against using the towpath after heavy rain, one corner of it lifting in the wind. She went down past it.

The bridge stood ahead with the rain bouncing off its rail. The pull started before the lock, low in her, as if the path tipped toward the water, and she kept her eyes on the lamps of the tram stop beyond it.

Her phone went in her coat. She got it out with her left hand. Mum.

"Natty? It's Mum."

"Mum."

"You got my letter. You never wrote back."

"I got it."

"Well? I've the back room made up. You'd come the week before, and stay."

"I don't know yet."

"You don't know." There was a television somewhere in the house in Port Ellery. "What's that noise? Is that the sea?"

"The canal. It's raining here."

"You're out in that? At this hour?"

"There's a drain wants clearing."

She was level with the bridge. Below the path, in the stonework under it, the iron hatch had the water at its bottom rim. The patch pulled the way it always pulled, and harder, with the water high.

"You always did," Mum said. "Out in all weathers. Your father used to say-"

Natalie's left foot went to the edge of the path. She meant to stop it there. The next one went over, onto the bank, and the wet grass took her straight down it, and the canal came up over her boots and her knees and her thighs, and stopped at her hips.

She put her left hand out for the stone of the bridge, because it was the only hand that would hold. The phone was in it. It went out of her fingers into the canal.

"Natty? I can hear water, where are-"

Then there was only the rain.

She stood at the hatch with the water at her hips, as she had stood twenty-one years ago. It was cold enough that the cold stopped being anything. The current leaned on her. She waited, because waiting was the part she knew, and listened up at the dark of the bridge for a voice calling down. Nobody called down. After a while she knew nobody would, and she got her left hand flat on the stone and her knee on the bank, and dragged herself out onto the path in four goes, her right hand held up against her chest out of the way.

Mum's number had been in the phone. She had never known it by heart. The letter had the address at the top, underlined twice, and no number at all.

At the tram stop the grate had gone under. The water stood brown over it to the kerb.

She sat in the shelter with her trousers running onto the concrete and took the letter out of the pocket inside her coat. It was dry against the lining. She read it under the shelter's light. Port Ellery, underlined twice. A Tuesday in August, the fourteenth. On the second page, under the paragraph about the jetty: So that's the 4th, love.

She turned it over. Mum had said the week before. A week, and her round would be walked by somebody, or by nobody, Tuesday and Friday at dawn, and the tram stop grate would go under in the first rain and stay under.

There was a pencil in her coat, an old one with the end chewed. She smoothed the envelope on her knee and wrote down the back of it, left-handed, as small as she could make it. Chandler's Row, the gutter. Bakery bins. Bell Street, drain mouth. Salter's Lane, the iron, from the top, after a thaw. She got to the tram stop and wrote from the kerb side, and pressed so hard on kerb that the lead went through the paper.

Chronicle

Isabel came to Linden Rise Centre at five to ten that morning, after a tram, a train and a bus, with two fingers of her right hand swollen purple under the gauze. At the culvert under the ring road she and Amara Nwosu, who was fifteen, tested a corrected reading on the dial by the bench, then went into the drain lake together. The static led them to the grate, where a thread came out and slid toward the box in Amara's hands. Isabel pulled Amara back by her hood. The box sank under the grate and could not be recovered.

Amara then called her staff and walked the culvert bank, where six threads came out and were burned or pinned. Isabel had stood closer to the bars than Amara and was not targeted. The signal dropped to a low hum under the ring road. Isabel said the heart could be left for another day. Amara wrote a figure on the back of her hand and went back up the path with Isabel to catch the bus.

In Kell's Lane, Natalie chalked a line along the wall from the downpipe, marking the seam that had crept toward the fire stair. Her right hand would no longer close past halfway, and she could not find the root under the stair. She left the chalk on the bottom step, with a question mark at the end of the line. Mrs Vella told Isabel, when she came for the key at half past four, that Natalie had been there. Isabel found the chalk in the rain, tried to photograph it, and redrew the line from memory in her left hand. Mrs Vella said she would ring the council's emergency line that night and kept the key.

At seven, Natalie went out to the tram stop grate, which was flooding. On the towpath near the bridge she fell into the canal and lost her phone. She climbed out at the hatch, sat in the tram shelter, and wrote directions for her round on the back of her letter from her mother in Port Ellery.
