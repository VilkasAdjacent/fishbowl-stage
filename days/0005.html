Day 5: The Key Went Back Into the Cardigan Pocket

Sunday 8 July 2046, morning

Water's over the stone by the lock. Walk the far side and keep the little ones off the wall.Text message, forwarded in the Linnet Lane group

Isabel came off the bus at the Linden Rise Centre at ten to ten and walked down toward the ring road with her right hand held flat against her ribs inside her jacket. The lid in Mrs Vella's cellar had come down on its first two fingers yesterday, and the hand wouldn't close past a loose curl. On the bus she had dropped her phone on the step trying to pay with it.

She had spent the whole way out working out what she'd do if Amara was already in the water to the knee. Grab her, was the answer, and she couldn't grab anything.

The sky sat low and grey over the roofs, but it was dry. The drain lake had gone back off the path since Thursday and left a line of mud and flattened reed. Amara Nwosu was on the bench by the culvert with the box in her lap, four or five metres back from the water, sitting very straight.

"You waited," Isabel said.

"You said not to go near the grate till you were here." Amara stood up. "I've been here since half nine. I haven't even pointed it."

The static was there anyway, faint, from under the road: the same bad line as Thursday, further off, behind the tarmac.

Isabel sat on the end of the bench, because standing over her felt wrong for this. "Remember what I said on Thursday? About walking up on one alone."

"Not alone," Amara said. "You said it twice."

"I can tell you some of why now." Isabel kept her eyes on the grate. "There's a lane by mine where I left a cut half done this week, and the root went under a block of flats. Yesterday I went down the cellar after it. On my own. My neighbour was at the top of the stairs, which doesn't count. I followed it right across the floor to a cover by the back wall, got the lid up, and it wasn't there. It was behind me. By the door, between me and the stairs." She took the hand out of her jacket and held it up. "Lid came down on this when I turned."

Amara looked at the gauze, and then at her. "Did it have nerves?"

"I took every one the night I left it."

"Then it shouldn't have been able to-"

"No. It shouldn't." Isabel put the hand away. "That's why you don't. Not for anything you can see coming."

Five years she'd carried that rule without the reason. She'd got the reason yesterday on one knee in the dark, and here she was handing it on inside a day to a kid who'd have it word for word by lunchtime. It was the first thing she'd taught out here that she was sure of.

"So nerves first only-"

"Nerves first is still first."

"Okay." Amara turned the box over. "Can I do the reading? I've worked out the pin. It sits high resting, so I read it here first, on dry ground, then at the bars, and take the difference off." She held it out level. The needle lay a hair above the bottom of the dial, just shy of the first felt-tip mark. "That's what nothing looks like now. I'll tell you before I move."

"I'm next to you," Isabel said. "Not behind. Next to. And when I say out, you come out."

"Okay. I'm calling mine. Then three steps to the bars."

Amara called her staff, and the pale green wood came into her hands smelling of sap, and she was taller than Isabel again, as she had been on Thursday, standing in the mud as if she had all morning. Isabel called her own. The copper ran warm down both forearms and the shape settled over her, and she took the staff in her left hand and laid her right flat against the wood, which was all it was good for.

They went in together. The water came over Isabel's boots and was cold through her socks at once. Amara tucked her staff under her arm and held the box to the third bar with both hands, and the needle went up, wandered, dropped, went up.

"It won't sit. It's my hands."

Isabel set the end of her staff against the bottom of the box and leaned, so the wood pinned it to the iron. Her copper ran down into the bars. The needle steadied. It climbed past the first mark, past the second, slowly, and stopped just short of the third.

"Third," Amara said. "Nearly. Less the pin, that's two and most of another."

"I see it."

Then the static moved. It came out from under the road along the length of the culvert toward them, louder at every metre, and stopped a metre in behind the bars, under the path they'd walked down on. On Thursday she had told Amara it would keep. Her copper had been in the iron the whole time Amara read.

It turned, away from her staff, toward the box and the girl holding it.

"Out," Isabel said. "Back to the bench. Now."

Amara's eyes were on the dial. "Wait, it's going up-"

Isabel took her staff off the box and reached across with her right hand for Amara's sleeve. Her fingers found the wet nylon and wouldn't close. They slid down to the cuff and off. To get a real hold she'd have to drop the staff, the only thing between the bars and either of them, and she kept it.

"Amara. Out."

"It's at the stop-"

Something came up between the third bar and the fourth: grey-white, as thick as a garden hose, blunt at the end. It came out of the dark of the pipe and lay across the back of Amara's right hand, where she had it on the top bar to steady the box.

Amara didn't move. Her eyes went from the dial to her hand and stayed there, and the box stayed against the bars. Isabel said her name again and got her left elbow, staff and all, against Amara's arm and shoved, and the girl rocked and came back to where she'd been, her boots set in the mud under the water. The thing lay across her hand for as long as it liked. Then it drew back through the bars by itself, and the needle fell, and only then did Amara step back, once, twice, and turn and wade for the bank with the hand held out in front of her.

Isabel laid her staff across the bars and leaned with everything in her one good arm, and felt it sit there, a metre in, warm, going nowhere.

She couldn't cut it. Left-handed, her shape already thinning at the edges, nobody beside her but a fifteen-year-old with a hand just opened. She held the staff on the bars until Amara was on the bench, and a little after, then let it go, and the cold came through her jeans to the shin.

On the bench Amara was out of hers too, back in her big school jacket, holding the hand out flat. Across the back of it, below the knuckles, from the first finger to the third, ran a scald as wide as a shoelace, copper-coloured, wet down the middle.

Isabel had gauze from the chemist. She couldn't start the roll left-handed, and Amara held the loose end on her knee with her other thumb while Isabel wound it, not tight.

On Thursday it had been under the road, out of anybody's reach. Now it was under the path out of the estate, and Isabel had put it there, or helped. She'd have to tell Natalie, and watch Natalie's face while she decided whether Linden Rise was Isabel's to come back to.

Amara watched the dial the whole time, not the hand. "Did you see where it got to? Before."

"Third mark."

"Nearly third." Amara tipped the box. The needle had gone back to where it started on the bench, off zero, pointing at nothing. "Less the pin."

Amara Nwosu walked back up past the Centre beside Isabel with her right hand in Isabel's gauze, held against her chest, and the box in her satchel knocking her hip. Out of her shape she was a head shorter again. Over the river, past the last of the roofs, the sky had gone nearly navy, and the wind had turned round and smelled of rain.

Two dressings now. Mum would want better than the iron for this one, and Amara would have it worked out by Rowan Crescent. First she wanted Isabel to say it. Three words would do.

"It sat still once you had your staff on it," she said. "First time it's ever held still at a grate. So the joint's good, and I took the pin off the reading. Two and most of a mark, at the bars."

"It read," Isabel said.

"It read true."

"It read something." Isabel was half a step ahead with her bad hand inside her jacket. "Then that came up the pipe. I said out and you were looking at the dial."

"You said out and it was already there. Like, a second."

"A second's what you get."

Amara's face went hot to the ears. Tell me before you move had a back half, come when I say, and she'd done the front half and thought it was the whole rule. She'd wanted one sentence and got the rule back instead. She said okay, and counted lamp posts to the car park, and got to seven.

"Your mum works for Second Growth," Isabel said. "You never said."

The tomato, then, somehow. Amara had turned it over the whole tram ride knowing it would go somewhere, and here it was, an hour out of town, in Isabel's mouth. She couldn't tell what else had come with it.

"She's running the thing on Tuesday," Amara said. "She didn't want me at home on my own." She shifted the satchel strap. "There's a woman with a stall by the Tangle gate. Tomatoes. Her name's on every pole down there."

Isabel looked at her sideways. "Hyejin."

"She gave me one. A tomato."

"She writes to new girls. She wrote to me, five years ago, when I was new. Three messages."

"What it costs," Amara said. "How girls die. Nobody's coming."

"Same three, then."

"Did you answer?"

"One."

"Which?"

"The last one." One corner of Isabel's mouth went flat. "I said somebody had."

Amara thought about that across the whole car park. Somebody had come for Isabel: Natalie, who was in every third thing Isabel said. Nobody had come for Amara but Isabel, on a train and a bus, with a hand she couldn't use. And Hyejin, from behind a trestle, with a tomato.

"She doesn't look like somebody who'd know how girls die," Amara said.

"That's hers to tell, not mine."

They reached the shelter. Isabel didn't sit.

"Your turn," she said. "You don't have to say how many. Thursday your feet knew that bank. Today you put your hand on the top bar like you've had it on bars before. Just yes or no."

"I've read every post on the forums back to forty-one," Amara said. "There's a girl up the coast who does all hers with a hand on the iron. She says it's steadier."

"That's not what I asked."

The bus came round the roundabout, BREEN'S CROSSING across the front. Isabel didn't move toward the kerb.

"That's yours," Amara said. "There isn't another for ages. It's going to rain."

"I'll get the next one."

Amara looked at the bus and not at her. If she said yes, the two cuts would be out, and so would the first message she ever sent Isabel: a girl who'd done it twice asking to be shown as if she never had. Isabel would know the first thing Amara said to her was a lie, and she might not get on any bus out here again. Then Natalie would know, and whoever Natalie told.

The doors opened. Nobody got off. The doors shut, and the bus pulled off down the road without them.

"I'll wait with you," Amara said. "Till the next one."

"Go home," Isabel said. "Put something proper on that before your mum sees it."

She sat down on the shelter's bench.

Amara went. At the corner of Rowan Crescent she looked back. Isabel was under the half of the shelter that had a roof, with her good hand on her knee, watching the road the bus had taken.

The rain had come in off the river after midday, and at half past three Natalie was standing in Kell's Lane with it running down the back of her collar. The lane was ten minutes from Weir Street and it wasn't hers. Iz was at Linden Rise, an hour out on the train.

Last night Natalie had told her to leave this lane until Natalie could come with her, and Iz had said okay and gone down the stairs into the rain. Natalie meant to come. But she wanted to know where the thing had got to first, so that when she brought Iz back here it would be to something Iz could do. And if she called her staff, a root that had felt a girl cross a cellar floor would feel her too, and go deeper, and Iz would come back to a lane with nothing left to find. So she kept the staff where it was and put her left hand flat on the wall by the downpipe.

Her right was no good for it. The scald across the base of that palm had opened on the Tangle's gate yesterday, and the fresh gauze had been wet through before she was halfway up Linnet Lane.

The knit was nearly over. She found it at the downpipe as a faint itch in the mortar, nothing much, a stitch you might feel through a sleeve, and moved her hand along it a hand's width at a time toward the fire stair. Rain sheeted down the brick carrying grit, and wherever the water ran thickest the itch went thinnest. Two metres. Three. A metre short of the stair it went out.

She crouched and put her fingers between the cobbles, in the running water. Nothing. Back to the wall at the last place she'd had it. Nothing there either. It had crept toward the stair since Friday, and from there it might be under the stair, or down the drain again, or along under somebody's step. She could tell Iz none of that. She could tell her she'd been.

A door opened behind her. Mrs Vella came out under a black umbrella, in a coat over her cardigan.

"You're the other one," she said. "From Friday."

"Natalie."

"Where's the young one?"

"Out at Linden Rise."

"Then it's you, love. Do it today." Mrs Vella tipped the umbrella at the lane. "They're inside with the rain, but the minute it stops they'll be out in every puddle on this lane, and every puddle on this lane's in a drain."

"It's Iz's to do."

"It's my lane."

"It is." Natalie took her hand off the wall. "Keep them in your yard. Off the drains on both lanes, and away from the stair. Till Iz is back to it."

"And when's that?"

"This week."

"This week. In ninety-eight it was this week. Seven weeks I had them digging, and the Grech boy poorly all year, and nobody could say with what. I'm not keeping the little ones in a yard on this week."

"If they're at that drain before she's done it, I can't tell you what it'll do to them," Natalie said. "I can tell you she's the one I'd send."

Mrs Vella took in the wet gauze, and the wall where the hand had been, and her weight came back on her heels. She'd stopped fighting and started bargaining.

"Wednesday," she said. "I'll keep them in till Wednesday. Thursday morning I'm on to the emergency line saying I can smell gas, and they'll have this lane up by dinner, and you can explain to them what's in the drain."

"I'll tell her it's Wednesday," Natalie said.

"And what is in the drain?"

"Something that got in at the old joint and likes it there. It doesn't like being looked for."

"That's not straight."

"It's as straight as I have."

Mrs Vella went in. Natalie walked down Linnet Lane counting it the way Iz would have to: Salter's Lane at five on Tuesday, if it thawed, then this lane by Wednesday with a root that could feel her coming, then the round on Friday, the chapel yard first because it froze first. All of it a tram apart, if you knew which tram. At the corner she turned south for the old core, and not for home.

It was gone six when Isabel got back to the north lanes, wet through from the walk from the station. Mrs Vella's cellar key was still in her jacket on its loop of string, where she'd put it yesterday instead of going in for the tea. She came into Kell's Lane the back way, before going up to Hoshi, because she'd said she'd come up and tell, and hadn't, and she wanted it done before Mrs Vella rang anybody. Mrs Vella was on her step under the overhang with the thermos, and the second chipped mug already poured: the tea Isabel had walked off from yesterday.

"There she is," Mrs Vella said. "You said you'd come up and tell me what you found, love. You came up and told me nothing, and walked off with my key."

"I've got it." Isabel held it out left-handed.

Mrs Vella held out the mug instead, and Isabel had to put the key away again to take it. "Your friend was here this afternoon. Stood at that wall the best part of an hour in the rain with one hand flat on the bricks. Never got her stick out, never knelt, never touched the drain. Left it be. Then she told me to keep the little ones in till Wednesday, and went."

Isabel held the mug against her chest.

Leave Kell's Lane till I can come with you. And then Natalie had come without her, the one day Isabel was a train away, and stood an hour and done nothing to it. Checking up. Leaving it there for Isabel the way she'd left Salter's Lane, to see what she'd make of it. And under that was the worse thing: she wanted Natalie here, on this step, more than she wanted to be angry about it. Last night she'd walked out rather than take the end of the sofa, and tonight she'd have stood in the rain an hour for the chance to be asked again.

"Wednesday," she said.

"Wednesday, she said, and then I ring them. So." Mrs Vella sat down on the step. "Now you tell me. Plain."

Isabel had put it in order on the train: true, and not frightening. "It's at the old joint under the cellar floor. Where the new pipe meets the brick. It's like damp that's got into the drain, only it moves along it. It's not the pipe. If the council dig it up they'll find a pipe and nothing wrong with it. I'll get it out."

"Something in the drain that moves." Mrs Vella said it slowly. "Makes the cellar smell every winter. And a boy at number four poorly a whole year with nobody able to say what."

"I didn't say-"

"You didn't have to, love." She held her hand out, flat. "Key."

Isabel gave it to her.

"I'm ringing in the morning. Wednesday or no Wednesday. Your friend never went down those steps and came up with her fingers like that." Mrs Vella put the key in her cardigan pocket. "If it's nothing, they'll find nothing, and I'll have wasted a phone call. I've wasted three already."

A council van in the lane, then, and men cutting the cobbles over a thing that could feel a girl crossing a floor. It would run, deeper, under the block, or somebody in a hi-vis vest would lift that cover with his back to the door. Isabel had nothing left to argue with. She drank the tea, which was stewed and much too sweet, gave back the mug, and went down the lane to the fire stair in the rain.

She crouched at the foot of it and put her left fingertips to the cobbles. The static took a long time to come, and when it came it was mostly hiss, dropping out for whole seconds. The knit had crept along the wall from where she'd lost it on Friday, almost closed now, a hair's width where the rain ran differently over the stones. She followed it the last metre. It went in under the bottom step of the fire stair, and she couldn't get her hand any further.

Natalie's hand had been on this wall for an hour. Isabel didn't know if she'd got this far. She didn't know about the morning.

Isabel got up, wiped her fingers on her jeans, and walked to the end of Linnet Lane for the 14 to Weir Street.

By eight the canal had come up the stone to a hand below the lip of the towpath, and Natalie was standing under the lamp at the corner where the lane came down from the Tangle.

She'd come round by the wall. Hyejin's flyers had gone to pulp on every pole, the vines run down into the paper, and the stall's shutter was down with the notice grey behind its window. On the gate, her own copper-brown smear from yesterday was still on the bar, half washed off.

She looked at the towpath as Iz would have to look at it at five on a Tuesday, in the dark, on her own, with the water up. You came down here from Salter's Lane. The stone by the lock was loose. You didn't stop under this lamp.

Her phone went in the inside pocket of her coat, against the letter. She got it out left-handed. The screen said Mum. She would keep her voice level and her feet on the stone, and get to the end of it.

"Natty."

"Hi, Mum."

"Did you get my letter?"

"I got it."

"You've not written back, so I'm ringing. It's the fourteenth, love. I've put your name down. You're my witness, I've written it on the form." Mum's voice came big down the line, the jetty still in it. "I told them you'd come."

The patch by the bridge was where it always was, under the near arch, and it pulled. Natalie said she'd read it, which was true. She said August was a long way off, which was true. The pull came low, like a floor tipping, and her feet went with it, one and then the next, down the wet grass of the bank with her weight back, while Mum talked about the jetty being redone and the man who was doing it.

The water was cold over her boots, then to her shins, then to the knee. Under the bridge the iron plate of the hatch stood out of the stonework with the water a hand below its rim.

"You'll come, won't you," Mum said. "Natty?"

"Mum, I-" Her voice went thin on it, and didn't come back.

"Where are you? Is that water? You're outside in this. I can hear it."

From the towpath above her someone said, "Nat?"

Iz was under the lamp, wet to the skin, her right hand held against her jacket.

"I'll ring you back," Natalie said into the phone.

"You won't."

"I will." She ended it, and put the phone back against the letter.

Iz crouched at the top of the bank and held out her left hand. Natalie gave her the left, the one that worked, and Iz leaned back, and Natalie came up the last of the grass with her boots full and her trousers dark to the thigh. Iz's shoulders were up round her ears. She had something ready.

"You weren't in," Iz said. "I came to Weir Street. Who was that?"

"Somebody I've said I'll ring back."

"Port Ellery?"

"When I'm dry."

"That's not a yes or a no."

Natalie pushed the phone down to the bottom of the pocket.

"And you take it down there," Iz said. "In the canal."

"It rang when it rang."

Iz let go of her hand. "Mrs Vella says you were on Kell's Lane this afternoon. With your hand on the wall for an hour."

"I was."

"You told me leave it till you could come with me."

"I didn't call my staff."

"That's not- why were you there?"

"I put my hand on the wall."

"That's what you did. Not why."

"I couldn't find it, Iz."

Iz stood with that, rain running off her hair. "I found it. It's gone under the bottom step of the fire stair. And she's ringing the council in the morning, not Wednesday. I told her it moves, and she took her key back."

Tomorrow, then. A van in the lane by nine, and men with a saw for the cobbles over a root that had already gone round Iz once in the dark. "Seven," Natalie said. "I'll meet you at the downpipe."

"You'll meet me." Iz said it flat. "Okay." She looked past Natalie at the bridge, at the water under it, and back. "If I'm walking your round Tuesday, I'm walking past this. I want to know what I'm walking past."

"A towpath that's shut after rain," Natalie said. "I read you the railing, the morning you found me here."

Iz nodded, once, and her weight was already on the balls of her feet. Then she turned and went off along the towpath toward the lock, the shut way, in the dark, with her bad hand against her ribs. Natalie went after her as far as the loose stone, slower in the wet boots, and watched her reach the steps up to Lockyer Street and climb them and go out of the light.

She went home by the street. At Weir Street she stood her boots on newspaper by the heater, toes to it, and took the letter out of her coat. It had gone soft through. She opened it flat on top of the heater to dry, both pages side by side, Mum's big loose writing coming up dark through the wet, and set her phone beside it, and sat on the floor in her wet trousers until the paper began to curl.

Chronicle

Isabel met Amara Nwosu at the culvert below Linden Rise a little before ten, her right hand held against her ribs. Amara had been waiting on the bench with the box. They called their staffs and went into the water together, and Isabel leaned her staff on the box so that the needle steadied just short of the third mark.

The static came along the culvert and stopped a metre behind the bars. Isabel told Amara to come out, but Amara was still watching the dial, and Isabel could not close her hand on Amara's sleeve. Something came up between the third and fourth bars and lay across the back of Amara's right hand. Isabel shoved her with her staff and elbow, and the thing drew back through the bars on its own. Amara waded for the bank.

Isabel held her staff across the bars until Amara was on the bench, then let it go. Amara had a scald from her first finger to her third, and Isabel wound gauze over it one-handed. The bus came and went without them, and Amara walked home. Isabel stayed on the shelter bench.

In the afternoon Natalie stood in Kell's Lane in the rain, one hand flat on the wall by the downpipe, and found the knit as a faint itch in the mortar. She followed it toward the fire stair and lost it a metre short. Mrs Vella agreed to keep the children in until Wednesday.

After six Isabel told Mrs Vella that the thing was under the cellar floor where the new pipe met the brick. Mrs Vella took back her key and said she would ring the council in the morning. Isabel followed the knit along the wall to the fire stair, where it went in under the bottom step.

At eight the canal stood a hand below the towpath. Natalie was on the phone to her mother when the bank under the bridge pulled at her, and she went down the wet grass into the water to the knee. Isabel took her left hand on the towpath and leaned back until Natalie came up the bank. Isabel said she had found the thing under the fire stair. They agreed to meet at the downpipe at seven the next morning.
