Day 5: The Chalk Cross the Rain Took Before Anyone Could Read It

Sunday 8 July 2046, morning

Vote YES on the Linden Rise drainage levy: clean water, clean lanes, sound pipes for every street.Printed leaflet, Linden Rise ward referendum

Amara Nwosu was at the culvert by twenty to ten on Sunday, on the dry side of the drain lake, her boots where the water stopped and the meter buckled into her satchel. The sky over Linden Rise was flat grey. Every so often a lorry went over the ring road on its pillars with a sound like a wardrobe dragged across a floor upstairs. The lake still lay over the path by the grate, and more of the reeds at the culvert mouth had gone black at the root than on Thursday.

She hadn't gone near. Isabel's text had said not to, so she had stood here twenty minutes doing what it said, with the signal behind her teeth like a drill in somebody else's flat. It was sharper than Thursday. When she turned her head toward the grate it sharpened again, and when she turned away it eased, as if it had a direction and the direction was her.

The plan was one reading she could trust, with Isabel watching, and Isabel saying it was right. What she'd turned over the whole way down the crescent was the other thing: that Isabel would ask her something too late for any excuse to hold.

Isabel came down the path from the Centre at five to, off the train at Breen's Crossing and the bus out, in the thin black jacket, her right hand flat inside the front of it against her ribs. The gauze showed at the cuff, browner than Thursday, and the first two fingers were strapped together.

"You waited," Isabel said.

"You said to."

Isabel stood and listened with her head on one side. "It's louder."

"It was louder Thursday. Today it's sharper." Amara heard herself. "Is that a dumb thing to say?"

"No. Sharper's right." Isabel looked at the grate, not at her. "Okay. Two things, before anything. Nerves first. If it's grown nerves you take them before you go near the rest, or it feels you and runs. And you never walk up on one alone."

"I'm not alone. You're here."

"That's why I'm here." Isabel's left thumb went along the strapped fingers through the jacket. "Natalie told me both of those my first night with a staff. She never said why about the second one. I found out yesterday. There was one under a block near mine, in the cellar, and I went down on my own with my back to the door, and it came up behind me. Between me and the stairs." She stopped there. "It waited till I was facing the wrong way."

"Did it get you?"

"It went round me." She took the hand out long enough to show it, and put it back. "So. One of you looks at it. One of you looks behind. Whoever's looking behind says when it moves."

Amara had it whole the first time, like the last line of a proof. "One looks at it, one looks behind, the one behind says when it moves. And the one in front says before she moves."

"Yeah." Isabel looked at her properly then. "Thursday your feet knew that bank. You went along it like you'd stood at one before. Have you? Not this one. Any."

There it was, early, and still too late for an excuse. "I come down here loads. It's on the way back from school." Amara took the meter out. "And I've read everything. There's a girl in Corrigan on the forums who drew diagrams of banks."

"That's not what I asked."

"I know the bank because I've walked the bank." She held up the box. "Can I do it now? I'll tell you before every step."

Isabel's mouth flattened, and she nodded at the water.

Amara called her staff. It came green into her hand, smelling of sap, and the copper ran warm down both forearms like the inside of a two-pence piece held to a lamp, and her shape came over her. From up here the lake looked smaller. Isabel came to her chin. Nothing anywhere was in a hurry, not the lorries, not the water, not her.

Isabel didn't call hers yet. She stood a step behind on the path, half turned, so she had the grate and the path back to the bus stop both.

The needle rested where it always rested now, a hair above zero, off the bent pin. Amara had done the sums last night: take that off whatever it read, and what was left was true. "That's rest," she said. "Two steps. To the edge."

"Go."

Two steps. The needle lifted to the first mark.

"One more. In to my ankle. Then I hold it out and I don't move my feet."

"Okay."

The water came cold over her boot. The needle climbed to the second mark, and the third, and the signal thinned to a bright wire, and behind the grate the thing turned. Not toward Isabel. Toward her.

"It's turned," she said. "It's facing me."

"Then come back. Say it and come back."

The needle was at the last mark and still climbing. If it stopped short of the stop, she could subtract and know. If it hit the stop, it was the pin or it was the thing, and she'd never know which, and Thursday's reading had ended the same way, rattling in the bottom of the box. One more second, and she'd have a number to show, and Isabel would have to say it was right.

"I'm holding it out," she said. "Feet still." She put the box toward the bars at the full length of her arm.

The needle hit the stop and stayed.

A thread came out between the bars, pale and finger-thin, fast along the top of the water, and laid itself across the back of her right hand where it held the box. She knew what it was before it hurt. Then it hurt.

Isabel called hers on the move. The pale staff was in the water beside Amara's before Amara heard her step off the path. She had it in her left hand only, and she laid it across the thread where it lay on Amara's skin and leaned, and the thread turned grey and came off into the lake in flakes. Under the road the rest of it drew back, the signal thinning, then far.

Isabel's shape was gone off her at once. She was down on one knee in the shallows with nothing in her hands, her right hand pressed into her stomach, the lake over the gauze.

Amara backed on to the path and let her own shape go. The scald ran from her first knuckle to the wrist bone, as wide as a shoelace, copper-coloured, wet down the middle. She shook the box. The needle came off the stop and fell back to a hair above zero.

So it had gone to the end, and the end was only the end. She had a new scald for a reading that said nothing.

Isabel got up out of the water soaked to the thigh and looked at Amara's hand.

"It went for you," she said. "I was right there, and it went past me for you. Again." She was breathing hard. "Amara. Have you done a cut before? On your own. Yes or no."

"I said before I moved. Every time. You heard me."

"You did."

That was nearly it. Amara waited for the rest, for that was right, and Isabel was already past it.

"That's not what I'm asking."

"I never got near it. You said nerves first, and I wasn't close enough to touch one."

"Your mum's Second Growth," Isabel said. "Hyejin told somebody you had this at the Tangle yesterday."

So Hyejin had said it after all, to someone, who'd said it to Isabel. All Amara had left was her face, and she kept it still.

"Mum runs consultations. She plans the rooms." She put the box in the satchel. "It's for physics. Interference."

Isabel looked at her, then up at the ring road, where a bus was coming round the long bend toward the Centre. Amara watched her work something out, and watched it come out against her.

"That's the twelve-ten," Isabel said. "I'll get that one. Next time tell me what you already know, first. Then I'll know what I'm teaching."

"Okay."

They went up the path together. Out of the shape Amara was shorter than Isabel again, level with her ear, the school jacket loose at the shoulders.

"You were taller than me down there," Isabel said.

"I'm still growing," Amara said. "Mum bought the jacket big for it."

Isabel looked at her sideways, and then at the bus.

"I'll walk you to the stop," Amara said, and Isabel let her.

Natalie came into Kell's Lane from the Linnet Lane end a little after twelve, under a sky gone low, with the river smell coming ahead of the rain. Iz would be at Linden Rise by now, on the far side of the ring road, on a bank beside the Second Growth woman's girl. Mrs Vella was out on her back step with the thermos before Natalie was halfway down.

"You're the other one," Mrs Vella said. "From Friday."

"Natalie."

"She's not here. Went off for the train this morning with a bag."

"I know."

The seam had nearly gone. On Friday it had run crooked a metre back from the downpipe; today the mortar there had closed and dried, and if Natalie hadn't watched it close she'd have walked past. What was left lay further along the foot of the wall, fainter, and it had crept since Friday a hand at a time toward the fire stair. The itch was hardly there. She stood still on the cobbles and waited for it, and it came and went like a radio between stations.

She only wanted to know how far it had got, so Iz wouldn't come back to it blind with a hand that didn't close. If she called her staff and opened it, she'd be on her knees in this lane holding it, and it would be hers again, and Iz would have it from Mrs Vella by teatime. It was a thing to find the end of, and leave.

She got down by the wall.

Her right palm was under fresh gauze, the Salter's Lane scald torn open in the middle on the Tangle's gate yesterday, and through the gauze the stones felt of nothing. She peeled the dressing back off the heel of her hand and laid the palm flat on the mortar.

The knit was there, a warmth under the cold a finger wide. She followed it a hand's width at a time. It didn't run straight. It wandered up into the second course of bricks and down again, and twice it dropped out and she had to go back to where she'd last had it and come at it again. The second time the soft middle of the scald opened on the grit.

"Who's coming to see to it?" Mrs Vella said.

"It's slow," Natalie said. "It won't do much this week."

"That's not who."

"Someone who knows the lane."

"Her, you mean. With the hand." Mrs Vella poured a mug and kept it. "When?"

"When the hand's better."

"And till then I keep the little ones off three drains and a downpipe, on the say of two girls who won't tell me what's in them." She set the mug on the step beside her. "The council's been saying someone'll come since Tuesday. I'd like it from a person, once."

Natalie lifted her hand. It had left copper-brown in the grit. She put it back. "Keep them off the drains. Both lanes. That's the most use anybody can be to you this week."

"You're as bad as she is."

A metre short of the fire stair the knit dropped out under her palm and didn't come back. She waited. She moved her hand right along the brick, and left, and down to the join with the cobbles, and there was the cold of the stone and the grit in the wet of the scald and nothing else. It might have gone on under the stair, or into the wall, or down. With the palm like this she couldn't tell which, and to find out she'd have to call the staff.

She sat back on her heels. The gauze wouldn't go back on; it slid on the wet and folded over on itself, and she left it hanging off her wrist.

On the step, among the things the little ones from number nine had left, there was a tin of coloured chalks. "Can I borrow one of those?"

Mrs Vella pushed the tin along with her foot. Natalie took the white in her left hand, which didn't know how, and drew a cross on the bottom brick by the fire stair, low, where the knit had dropped out. One arm came out longer than the other. It was the mark she put on a wall on her round when a thing wanted coming back to. Iz had walked five years behind her watching her put it there.

"What's that when it's at home?"

"Where it got to."

"I'll tell her you were here, then," Mrs Vella said, "since you won't." She held out the mug.

Natalie took it left-handed and drank it standing, too hot and too sweet, the right hand open at her side. If she went home for a week, there'd be a good many crosses for Iz to read.

She gave the mug back and went up the lane ahead of the rain, the chalk still in her left hand.

The rain had come in off the river by the time Isabel got off the train, and at half past four Kell's Lane was running with it, the cobbles shining, her jeans wet from the lake to the thigh and wet again now from the knee. Her right hand had nothing left in it. The strapped fingers wouldn't bend, and somewhere on the train the rest of the hand had stopped being any use. She'd promised Mrs Vella the cellar over a cup of tea and she'd come to keep it, and she'd decided on the walk up not to go near the wall. Natalie had said leave it.

Mrs Vella had her coat on and came out under the little porch over her step before Isabel reached the downpipe.

"Your Natalie was here," she said. "Dinnertime. Half an hour on her knees with her hand on the wall. She's left you something."

At the foot of the fire stair, on the bottom brick, was a chalk cross, white and crooked, one arm longer than the other. The rain had got to it already.

Isabel knew that cross. Five years of dawn rounds behind Natalie, and that was what went on a wall when Natalie meant to come back: the gutter at Chandler's Row, the chapel yard before a frost. Natalie's mark, for Natalie. Leave Kell's Lane till I can come with you, and then she'd come without her, the one morning Isabel was a train-line off.

Or it was for Isabel, like a note left on the fridge. She couldn't tell from here, and she wasn't going to ask Mrs Vella what Natalie's face had been doing while she drew it. If it marked something, she could at least hear what.

She crouched by the cross with her bad hand against her chest, shut her eyes, and listened for the static.

Fainter than any day this week. The rain was in everything, on the cobbles, in the downpipe, off the fire stair in a long thin stream, and the static was under all of it if it was there at all, one word in ten on a bad line. She got down on her knees on the wet stones and put her ear nearer the bottom course of bricks. There: a breath of it, toward the stair. Then nothing. She shuffled a hand's width along and listened again, and the downpipe gurgled over it, and when the downpipe stopped there was only rain.

With her staff the line would have come up under her like a road under headlights. But at the culvert this morning she'd had the wood in her left hand and the right pressed into her stomach, and she'd held it seconds. She stayed on her knees.

When she opened her eyes the cross had run. The long arm was a white streak down the brick and the short one a smudge. Whatever place Natalie had meant, it wasn't on the wall any more.

"You'll catch your death," Mrs Vella said. "In. Now."

The kitchen was hot, the radio low, a plate of biscuits nobody had asked for. Isabel sat at the table with her jacket dripping on the lino and the mug in her left hand.

"The cellar," Mrs Vella said. "You promised."

She'd tried ways of saying it on the train and none had come out right, so she said it plain. "There's something in the drain. Not rats. Not the pipe. It sits where the new pipe joins the old one. It was probably there in ninety-eight. It's why the cellar smells."

"Then that's the council's."

"The council can't see it. They'll send a man with a camera and he'll see a stain."

"And you can see it."

"Yeah."

Mrs Vella looked at the gauze on the table. "The Grech boy."

"I don't know," Isabel said. "Maybe." She put the mug down. "It's not where it was, though. So it's every drain on both lanes, not just the downpipe. Till I say."

Mrs Vella sat with both hands round her own mug, and Isabel watched her go back through forty years of the lane and find the year, and the boy, and believe it.

"Till you say," Mrs Vella said. "Then you'll say. Every night, six o'clock, at that door, where it's got to. I'm not having it off a chalk mark."

"I could text you."

"I don't text. At that door, or I'm on to the council again in the morning, and the paper after, and they can have the lane up like ninety-eight."

Men with spades in the lane, and the thing under it feeling every one of them. Six, every night. Isabel saw it go into the week before she could stop it: six here, and Linden Rise whenever Amara texted, cut short to make the train back, and five on Tuesday at Salter's Lane, and whatever was left over for Weir Street, which last night had been nothing, because she'd said no to it, and now every evening there'd be this door first.

"Six," she said.

She drank the tea, which was stewed, and ate a biscuit because Mrs Vella was watching her not eat one. Ten past five by Mrs Vella's clock. In rain like this the tram stop grate went under first, and Natalie would be out to it by now, and home after along the towpath, because it was the short way. The towpath went under the bridge.

Isabel put her jacket on wet and went out into it.

The long grate under the tram stop was under a hand of brown water by six, and Natalie did it from the kerb side, kneeling, left-handed, as she had yesterday. In the shelter, with the rain loud on the roof, she took her mother's letter out and looked at the fourteenth on the first page and the fourth on the second, and put it back against the lining before the wet could get to it.

She went back along the Tangle's wall. The flyers had gone to pulp on the poles, the drawn vines running down them in green streaks, and the stall's shutter was down. Past the gate the wall ended and the towpath began, and the canal was over its lip, a skin of water across the gravel with the current pulling at it.

She could have gone up Lockyer Street and round. But her boots had been wet since Friday, and the towpath was the short way home, and she meant to stay on the gravel the whole length of it.

Her phone went in her coat as she came level with the bridge. Mum, the screen said: her mother, Ruth, in Port Ellery.

"Natty. You got my letter."

"I got it, Mum."

"You've not written back."

"I was going to."

"Don't write. Say. Are you coming?"

The patch was under the bridge where it always was, and it pulled. The water was up to the lower rim of the hatch, brown and moving. Natalie kept her eyes on the far bank.

"I've a round, Mum. Twice a week. Somebody has to walk it."

"Then get somebody. You've had thirty years to find somebody." Ruth's voice was louder than Natalie remembered, and higher. "I've put you down. It's written. You can't say no to a thing once it's written."

"I haven't said no."

"You haven't said anything."

The pull came low and steady, a floor tipping toward the water. She fixed on the council notice cable-tied to the railing across the canal, warning against the towpath after heavy rain, and answered her mother, and somewhere between one line and the next she was on the grass at the top of the bank, and then going down it sideways with her weight on her heels and the phone at her ear. The water came over her boots and up through the stiff trouser leg to her knees. Her left hand was flat on the iron plate of the hatch.

"Nat."

Iz was on the towpath above her, soaked through, her jacket stuck to her, the bad hand held against her chest. She had come from the lock end, where anyone would come from who knew which grate flooded first and how long Natalie took over it. She was looking at the hand on the hatch.

Natalie took her hand off the iron. That was all she could do about it now.

"Who's that?" Ruth said. "Is someone with you?"

"A friend, Mum."

Iz's face went still under the bridge lamp. Natalie heard Elspeth in it half a second after Iz did.

"Natty, I want you there for it. I want you to see me do it properly."

"I'll ring you back. Tonight."

"You won't."

"I will." She ended the call with her right thumb, and the scald across her palm caught on the edge of the case and opened again.

"Get out of there," Iz said.

The bank was steeper going up. Iz came down two steps and held out her left hand, and Natalie took it with hers, and Iz pulled, and her foot slid on the wet grass and her knee went into the mud, and she was still holding on. Natalie came up past her on to the gravel. Water ran out of her boots.

"That was your mum," Iz said, still on her knee.

"Yes."

"And the letter."

"It's from home."

"What does it say? Is that August? Is that what Tuesday was?"

"Tuesday's Salter's Lane."

In her coat the phone went again. Natalie put her hand flat over the pocket and kept it there, the thing buzzing against the open scald, until it stopped.

"You're not going to get that."

"I said tonight."

"Tonight for what?" Iz got up. "And who walks your round, if you go wherever she wants you? Because it's me, isn't it, it was always going to be me, you just haven't asked." She stopped. "A friend."

"I wasn't going to explain you to her down a phone, on a towpath."

It answered the last thing Iz had said and nothing before it, and Natalie watched her know it.

"Your cross on Kell's Lane ran," Iz said. "I couldn't hear a thing, and then it was off the brick before I'd worked out what it was for. You told me to leave that lane till you could come with me, and then you went without me." She was breathing hard. "So I'll find it, and I'll go down after it, and you'll be under the stair, and my name still won't go in the book."

Natalie looked at her. Iz's shoulders were up round her ears, the way they were at four in the morning on the round when she'd decided she was cold and wasn't going to say so. There was mud to her knee, and the gauze on her hand was grey with rain. Elspeth was still holding the Hundred and Fourth. It was the one true thing Natalie could put in Iz's hands tonight.

"Have you still got the jar?" Natalie asked.

Iz stared at her. "In the drawer. Clean."

"Keep it there."

They went along the towpath toward the lock with the water over their boots. At the bridge Iz stepped round behind her to the canal side, so that she was between Natalie and the water, and walked there all the way to the lock.
