Day 19: The Machine That Goes In Under the Science Block

Monday 16 July 2046, morning

Drainage works at Linden Rise School begin 22 July. Residents need take no action.a public-health notice on the eastern wards community feed

Next morning Hyejin Park was still on the cold flags against the old woman's front wall in the eastern wards when the community feed put a public-health story on her phone. A contractor's crawler, a camera on tracks, would go into the culvert under the Linden Rise School science block on the 22nd. The photograph was the drain lake from the Bay Sightings thread. For eleven years nobody had asked her what was under there; now it was on a works order. The story was public, and the school thought it was drainage. A warning from her would make the telling hers. She dismissed it and turned the phone face down.

The seam came out of the culvert mouth and crossed the lane crooked, the two cut edges of it sitting apart where a close should have run them together, and the frost had found the join in the night and gone down into it and set. Fourth windless morning. Nothing in the north lanes was in a hurry to thaw, and the serrated tussock held its crooked line the way a thing holds when it has been done on purpose.

Isabel walked the seam from the culvert end to the fence and back, reading it as she read a route: how far it ran, what it would take, how long the weather would let her stand at it. The Hundred and Fourth, serrated tussock, which made it sound as though a hundred and three others had earned their numbers first. Her hands shook inside her cuffs. They had shaken since the hatch, and the cold had come in on top of the shake until the two were doing the same work, and the knuckles had split again across the tops.

Footsteps came up the lane from the low crossing: Elspeth Hale, with the register under one arm. She shifted it to her other side, and then looked down at the hand that had held it, to check it had let go.

"Isabel. You've been at this since the frost went hard."

"Checking the cut."

"Have you eaten?"

"There's food at the flat."

Elspeth let that pass. "The lane is a cold place to check anything. You've no coat."

"The call this morning," Isabel said. "Who did it name?"

"Miss Marchetti, from Continuity. It was routine. There was no second name on it."

She said it the way she read an entry out from the step, the words already in their order, and she was answering before the question had finished coming out of Isabel's mouth, which was the one thing Elspeth Hale never did.

There had been a name on that call and the old woman had left it out without a stumble. If the name was Natalie's, then the reason it was on a file was the hands — a cut left half done in the north lanes and somebody taking it up and paying for it. A name meant Continuity had her, and it meant the having was Isabel's.

"Natalie took a cut out of my hands," she said. "If the file has her in it, I want to hear it from you."

"If there were a name," Elspeth said, "you are not the one I would read it out to."

That was the second answer, and it came as smooth as the first. Two ways to take it. Say the name back at her twice more, make her keep not saying it, and then she would want the hands shown — the old woman's, to see what they were holding, and Isabel's, to see what they had done — and the register would come open on the step before either of them got down the lane. Or say the cut was all it was, and stand here while an old woman who had just lied to her looked at the thing she could not finish.

"It's only the cut," Isabel said. "Checking it's holding. Go on up, Miss Hale, the glare's coming and you'll want your step."

"Mind it goes in if it moves," Elspeth said. "That's what the register is for."

"It won't move."

Elspeth went up the lane with the register against her side. At the step she stopped and did not look back down.

Isabel waited until the register was open on the old woman's knees. Nothing stood over the work now, and she had asked for that herself.

She put out her hand and the staff came to it — pale wood out of the cold air, copper running its length and along both forearms to the elbow. She stood at the crooked end of the seam and brought the shape up along the blade. It came the way it always came, and stood, and slid off into the ash.

She called it up again. It came, and stood, and went, and she kept the light along her arms a beat past the point where it was doing anything, the way she did, because a thing held was a thing not gone yet. Her fingers would not close on the wood properly. The staff lay warm under them and her grip came and went along it, and the shape came up a third time and slipped, and the copper stood along both arms to the crook, and she held it until her fingers opened.

The staff went down into the ash where the seam ran crooked, and the copper left it, and the wood lay pale in the frost.

The cut stayed half done, and she did not reach for the staff again.

By nine on the third morning Natalie had learned every foot on Isabel's stair except Isabel's. Mrs Vella's slippers from the ground floor, two treads at a time and a rest. The man underneath, who took the fire stair to smoke. The post, which didn't come up at all and only shook the gate. She had sat in Iz's chair in Iz's flat in the old core for two nights and most of two days, with the lamp on and the cat on the sill, and each of them had come up or gone down and none of them had stopped at the door.

She wanted to stop listening. It wasn't much to want, and she couldn't do it in here. In here every sound was somebody, until it wasn't.

The lamp was still on. In the glare off the frost it made a small amber circle on the floorboards that the day hadn't bothered to put out. She switched it off, and the flat went the colour of the window.

Hoshi wanted feeding. Natalie held the box against her chest and tipped it, because her hands were wrapped to the second knuckle in kitchen towel and tape and the burns across both palms had opened in the night again. Half the biscuits went in the bowl and half went across the lino. Hoshi ate the floor ones first.

Iz's phone was on the arm of the chair, face up, where it had been since Saturday. She had been through it then: messages, map, the lot. There was nothing in it about where Iz had gone. Natalie picked it up anyway, between the heels of her hands, and put it in her coat pocket. If Iz rang anyone from a borrowed phone, she'd ring her own number, because it was the only one she knew by heart. Then whoever answered ought to be somebody.

Iz's coat was on the hook by the bathroom door. Natalie looked at it, and left it.

At the door she stopped with the key. Leave it on the latch, and Iz could come up the stair and walk straight in to her coat. So could the canvasser with the leaflets, who had asked this stair for its old ones and might ask again, and so could the old woman with the register, who had found the door once by following the green on the ash and would find it as easily twice. Give the key to Mrs Vella, and Mrs Vella would bring it back up with a cup of tea and a question, and answer the next person who asked her as kindly as she answered everyone. Lock it and keep the key, and Iz came home to a shut door.

Iz had never once come home before four. Natalie had two nights of listening to say so.

She locked it. The key wanted a grip her fingers didn't have, so she set the bow against the side of her wrist and leaned, as she'd lean on the butt of a staff to drive it into a patch, and it went over with a clack she heard all the way down the stair. Behind the door Hoshi said something about it.

The fire stair was white. She went down it sideways with her elbow hooked over the rail rather than her hand, one tread and the next, and at the bottom the flowers on the ash under the stair were furred with frost and holding their colour. She didn't look at them long. Somebody would have to walk the lane before the blight came for that ground, and it wasn't going to be today.

Her own flat was four streets up, towards the canal. She hadn't been in it since Iz went out without a coat.

The cold had been in her since four in the morning on Saturday and had never properly gone out, only sat lower when she was indoors. Now it came up to meet her. It went into the towel first. The towel had been damp from the night and it stiffened on her hands inside a street, until she was carrying two cold parcels at the ends of her arms and the burns underneath them were the only warm thing she owned. She put them in her pockets. The right one found Iz's phone and stayed on it.

At the first corner she took it out and looked. The screen came up blue: the time, the date, a picture of the river Iz had taken in the summer. Nothing else. She put it away.

On the second street the cold went into her knees. She was forty-seven, stopped at eighteen, and her legs had always been the part of her that kept to the eighteen, that ran the bank and the towpath and the stair while the rest of her was arguing. They had walked her out into the rain on Saturday and up the iced canal steps on Monday, and they were doing it again now, slower, and then a good deal slower, in a way she had no word for because they had never done it before.

She told herself it was the frost on the pavement and shortened her step.

The laundrette on the corner of the second street was shut, and its step was a slab of concrete under a dry overhang with the shutter down behind it. She meant to stand by it a minute. Instead she sat, and it didn't feel like deciding; her knees went and the step was there.

All right. A minute.

The glare was full on the street now, pale and flat, and nothing in it was dripping. A tram went by at the end of the road with its windows white inside. A woman crossed with a dog in a coat and didn't look at her, which was fair; Natalie wouldn't have looked at herself either.

She tried the getting-up the usual way, hands on her knees and push. The hands wouldn't take her weight through the towel; the left one opened a little further across the palm and she felt the wet come through the wrap and go cold almost at once. She tried it with her elbows on her knees, and her legs listened to the request and declined it. She sat back against the shutter.

If Iz came up the stair now she'd find the door she'd walked out of shut against her, her coat on the hook inside it, and nothing she could ring from to say she was there, because the thing she'd ring was in Natalie's pocket two streets away.

Natalie got the phone out again with the heels of her hands and held it on her knee. Blue screen, the river, the time. She watched the minute change.

Both keys were in her pocket, Iz's and her own, and she was sitting on a laundrette step two streets from each door they opened, and she could not get up to go to either.

Chronicle

Next morning, against the old woman's wall in the eastern wards, Hyejin Park read a public-health story on the community feed. A contractor's crawler, a camera on tracks, would go under the Linden Rise School science block on the 22nd. The photograph was the drain lake.

The sightings-thread lake. Eleven years unasked, and now the thing sat under a science block on a works order.

The story was public; the school thought it drainage. She dismissed it and turned the phone face down, so the telling would not be hers.

In the north lanes Isabel asked Elspeth Hale who the city's call had named.

"Routine, Isabel. No second name." Elspeth said it to keep Natalie out, and it came out practised. Isabel heard that.

"I'm fine. Only checking the cut." She waved Elspeth up the lane, so no liar would stand over it.

Her staff came, light up both forearms. The shape would not hold to the blade. She held it anyway, until her fingers opened and the staff fell into the ash.

Natalie quit the vigil at Isabel's flat, to stop listening for the stair. She locked it from outside, Iz's phone in her pocket, and set off home. The cold sat her down two streets short. Iz would come back to a locked door; Natalie was in neither flat.
