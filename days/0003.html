Day 3: The Wall Closed Behind the Staff Before the Girl Was Called Off It

Friday 6 July 2046, morning

Tell me before you move. Don't leave it to the last minute.Scrawled on a fridge magnet note, Rowan Crescent

Natalie let herself out of her flat on Weir Street at a quarter to five and went down into the old core to walk her Friday round, into the first hard frost of the winter.

Everything had gone white in the night. The bluestone kerbs were furred with it, the cobbles in the lanes had a skin that took the print of her boots, and her breath stood in front of her face and stayed there. She had walked this round on Tuesdays and Fridays for twenty-nine years. Her feet took the corners before she did.

The first one was in the gutter at the foot of Chandler's Row, where it always came back: a grey stain on the stone the size of a dinner plate, warm enough underneath that the frost had gone off it in a ring. The itch sat faint at the back of her head. She called her staff, set the end of it on the stain and leaned, and the copper went out of her arms down the wood and into it, and it went to ash. She scuffed the ash into the crack between two stones and walked on. Four minutes.

The second was behind the bakery bins, under the downpipe, no bigger than a fist. Two minutes. A third, hardly anything, sat in the drain mouth on Bell Street. She did them the way Mum used to do the beans on the back step in Port Ellery: bent over, not looking up, moving along the row.

The grate in Salter's Lane was another thing. The itch was down in the iron. The cold had driven the blight into the bars the way it had stiffened the puddles, and when she set her staff on the grate and leaned, the copper went down the wood, stopped at the iron, and came back up her arms cold. She leaned again, harder, through her five minutes and then through five more. The frost went off the bars where the staff touched them and stayed white everywhere else.

At about the twelfth minute it bit. When she took her right hand off the staff there was a scald across the base of her palm, over the callus, a thumb wide and two thumbs long, copper-coloured, the skin gone wet and soft in the middle of the hard.

She looked at the grate. The root was still in the iron. It would sit there until a thaw loosened it, and then it would come up warm and want doing properly. She let the staff go and walked on with the hand open in the cold.

The round took her along the Tangle's wall next, the long brick side of it, with the trees standing up over the top much bigger than twenty-one years should have made them, white to the last twig. Every pole along the footpath had one of Hyejin's flyers on it, SAVE THE TANGLE over the hand-drawn vine, and the frost had got into the paper so the vines stood out stiff. The stall beside the gate had its shutter down. In its window the council's notice had curled at the corners and frozen that way. Natalie read it as she passed: PUBLIC CONSULTATION. PROPOSED SUBSTRATE HALL, EAST EDGE OF SITE. TUESDAY 10 JULY, 6.30 PM, CANAL COMMUNITY ROOMS. ALL WELCOME.

She walked a little nearer the gate than she generally did, near enough that the cold came off its bars onto her coat, and then she was past it, as she had been past it every Tuesday and Friday for twenty-one years, since there had first been a wall here to walk past.

At the corner where the lane came out on the towpath she stopped under the lamp and took Mum's letter out of her coat with her left hand.

Mum's writing had gone big and loose since the last letter, ten years back. Port Ellery at the top, underlined twice. A Tuesday in August, the fourteenth, and would Natty come home for it, and stay the week before, and be there. Natalie read the first page through. On the second, near the bottom, after a paragraph about the jetty being redone, Mum had written: So that's the 4th, love. Don't leave it to the last minute like you do.

She looked at the fourth for a while. She turned back to the first page and found the fourteenth where she had left it. She folded the letter along its old creases and put it back against the lining.

Beyond the lamp the canal was up to the lip of the towpath, skinned with ice along both edges and black and moving down the middle. The bridge stood over it white along its rail. The patch by the bridge was where it always was, and it pulled.

She meant to walk past it. She had walked past it on Tuesday.

She went down the bank on the frozen grass with her weight back on her heels. At the bottom the ice took one step and not the second, and her left boot went through into the canal. The water came over the top of the boot and up to the middle of her shin.

She stood in it. Under the bridge, set into the stonework above the waterline, the iron plate of the hatch had frost along its rim. Once the water had come to her hips in there, and she had waited with it climbing and listened and trembled. She'd gotten good at waiting. She went to leave, and found the water had left her foot numb. She scrabbled back up the bank with her hands, the scald on her palm opening on the frozen grass.

Her boot squelched for the rest of the round, and her trouser leg froze stiff from the knee down. She did the two grates past the lock, and the one in the chapel yard, and the long one under the tram stop that flooded first in any rain. At each of them she stood a moment longer than they should have needed. The chapel yard froze before anywhere else did. The tram stop one wanted doing from the kerb side, not the road. Salter's Lane wanted coming back to after a thaw, at five in the morning, by somebody who knew to feel for it in the iron.

At twenty to seven she turned north for Kell's Lane with the frost still on her shoulders, and no time to go home and change her boots.

Isabel was in Kell's Lane by half past six, on her knees on the frozen cobbles, feeling for the seam with her left fingertips before she called anything. The pesto jar was in her jacket, washed twice, the label and the glue scrubbed off, the lid screwed down. Both scalds were under chemist's gauze, the one from the culvert and yesterday's from this lane, crossing each other on the back of her right hand. Overnight they had stuck to themselves, and the hand didn't close all the way anymore.

The static was fainter than yesterday. She had to hold her breath to hear it, a bad line behind her ear that dropped out for whole seconds, and she followed it under the fire stair and along the foot of the wall toward the downpipe, where yesterday it had nearly shouted. The frost lay over the whole lane the same, seam or no seam.

She had the order worked out from the walk over. Natalie would come in at the end of the lane and stand there. Isabel would open the wall from the downpipe end, come round under the stair, find the root and kill it, and Natalie would watch her do it. If Natalie got down on her knees under that stair, Isabel would spend the morning holding a wall for her, and she'd be back on Garnet Street's step with a jar that wasn't hers.

Mrs Vella had come out on her back step in a coat over her dressing gown, with a thermos and two chipped mugs on the step beside her. Somewhere behind the wall the little ones from number nine were hunting for puddle ice to stamp on, giggles echoing through the alley.

"I've told them this end's mine this morning," Mrs Vella said. "Is it the drains? It's been a fridge down here since Tuesday night. I've rung the council twice and got a recording."

"Ask me at eight," Isabel said.

Natalie came into the lane at seven. There was frost on her shoulders and in her hair at the part. Her left trouser leg was dark from the knee down and stiff with it, white along the crease, and her boot left wet prints that went white behind her.

"Straight from the round," she said, before Isabel could ask where from.

So she'd walked her round and gone down to that bank again, by the bridge, and come here with it frozen up her leg, and that was the whole answer she was going to get. Isabel put it aside for the moment.

"I want the stair end too," she said. "I've been here since half past. I've got the line. You can watch from Mrs Vella's step."

"Stair end's mine. We said last night."

"You said Elspeth's holding the Hundred and Fourth. If you kill the root, it's yours. She'll know. She knows everything."

"She writes whoever brings the jar. That's you."

"That's not the same and you know it's not." Isabel tried the other way in. "And you've been up since four, and you've got a foot full of ice."

"And you've a hand that doesn't close."

"It closes."

"Close it, then."

Isabel closed it. She got it most of the way, to where her nails nearly touched her palm, and the gauze over her knuckles went dark from the inside, copper-brown, spreading out to the edges. She opened it again.

Natalie didn't say anything about the gauze. "Wall's yours," she said. "All four metres. I'll be under the stair, and I'll be watching you the whole way."

Mrs Vella unscrewed the thermos and poured. Natalie took a mug and drank it standing, far too hot, the steam going up past her face. Mrs Vella held out the other.

"In a minute," Isabel said.

Then she called her staff. The pale wood came into her hands and the copper ran warm down both forearms, and the shape settled over her, and she knelt in it a moment with the staff across her knees before she put it to the wall. Her right hand wouldn't grip. She held the staff in her left and pressed the heel of the right against it, flat, which put the weight in the wrong place and a strain on the scalds. Better than no weight at all.

She started at the downpipe and leaned. Her copper ran out along the staff into the knit where the wall met the cobbles, and it opened under her: a line along the mortar where the frost wouldn't lie, the width of a finger, warm. She moved the staff along it a hand's width at a time and held what was behind her.

Natalie was on her knees under the fire stair with her own staff in the dark of it, turned so she could see the length of the wall. "Tell me when you're at the stair," she said.

One metre. Two. The static came up loud now, interference on every channel, all of it at the wall. Her right hand slid on the wood and she put her shoulder behind it instead. Somewhere past three metres her shape thinned on her like breath going off glass, and behind her, at the downpipe, she felt the seam start to close: the first hand's width, then the next.

She could call out now. Natalie was right there. She'd come out from under the stair and lay her staff along the wall beside Isabel's, and it would be Natalie's wall as well as Natalie's root, and Isabel would carry the jar to Garnet Street for both of them.

So she didn't. She leaned harder and took the staff on another hand's width toward the stair. She was still a meter off, and the cut was closing behind her faster than she was opening in front.

"There," Natalie said, under the stair, and went in.

The root didn't wait for her. Isabel felt it go past under the wall, along the knit behind her staff, the way she'd let close, fast and low, and down. Under the stair the end Natalie had pinned went grey. At the downpipe the static dropped into the drain grate at its foot and settled there, under the block, steady.

The shape slipped off Isabel and she was on her knees on the cobbles with nothing in her hands.

Goddammit. She'd pushed so hard, and it wasn't enough, she still would have lost it no matter whether Natalie had waited or not. The root would have gone the same way, down the knit she couldn't hold, and under. She'd washed a jar twice for this. She'd planned it on the walk over, again and again. Stupid. Her eyes stung, and she let them.

Whether Natalie had gone in because she'd felt the root move or because she'd seen Isabel's shape going, Isabel couldn't tell from the lane.

The seam ran crooked and closed from the downpipe back along the wall for about a metre, and went into the drain at the foot of the downpipe. The root was under the building now, somewhere along the drain, out of reach of anything she could do from the lane.

Natalie came out from under the stair on her elbows. On the cobbles where she'd been lay a handful of grey ash, dry on the frost. Isabel's left hand went to her jacket, to the jar, and she gripped it tight.

"Well?" Mrs Vella said, from the step. "It's not eight, but you've both stopped."

"No," Isabel said. "It's not safe. Keep them off the downpipe and the drain at the bottom of it, as well as the stair. It's gone into the drain under the block."

"Under the block. That'll be the whole lane up, then, just like ninety-eight."

Mrs Vella poured the second mug and held it out again, and this time Isabel got up and took it in her left hand. The tea was stewed and much too sweet. She drank it standing beside Natalie, with the empty jar against her ribs.

Amara Nwosu had the meter open on the kitchen table on Rowan Crescent by half past seven: the lid off, the magnifying lamp brought down from her desk and clamped to the table's edge, the soldering iron heating in its stand. The sleeve of her hoodie was pulled down over her left wrist and held there with her thumb. Mum's car was still out the front. Mum would be down by quarter to.

The needle had come off its pin at the culvert, and the pin had bent where it hit the stop. She straightened it with the small pliers under the lamp, by eye, a little at a time, faster than she should have. Then the joint. It was the one she'd thought it was: dull and grainy, the wire lifting off the pad when she touched it with the tweezers. A cold joint. She put the iron to it and fed in fresh solder, and it ran bright and smooth and set.

The needle was harder. It wouldn't sit. It dropped off the pin twice into the bottom of the box, and the second time Mum's feet were on the stairs. Amara got it back with the tweezers and pressed it on, and it went, and it sat. When she let go, it didn't fall to zero. It rested a hair above it, just shy of the first felt-tip mark, because the pin hadn't come quite straight. She tapped the box. The needle stayed where it was. So the box held together now, and whatever it read, it would read that much high. Doing the pin again meant taking the needle off, and there wasn't time. She pulled her sleeve straight.

Mum came in with her work blazer on and the Second Growth lanyard already round her neck, and a sheaf of papers under one arm. The top one said CONSULTATION EVENING, CANAL COMMUNITY ROOMS, TUESDAY 10 JULY. She put them on the bench and switched the kettle on.

"The chicken was in the sink when I got home," Mum said. "The water had gone warm."

"I forgot. Sorry. I'll do dinner tonight."

"You said you'd do it now. At twelve." Mum turned round and leaned on the bench. "So you weren't here at three. Where were you?"

"Out testing this." Amara tipped the box toward her. "It picks up interference. I wanted to see what it did away from the house."

"Away from the house where?"

"Round the crescent. The Centre."

Mum looked at her the way she looked at the news when a councillor was talking, quite still. "Your jeans are in the machine with drain lake on them to the knee," she said. "You were down at the culvert. I'm not cross about the lake. I want to know who you were with."

That was the question, and it was too late for any of the three excuses. They'd all been for the start. "On my own," Amara said.

"On your own in that water."

"It's not deep. I had boots on."

The iron ticked in its stand. Mum came over and reached for the plug at the wall. "You'll have the house down with that thing on and you looking at me." Amara reached for it too, to do it herself, and her sleeve rode up off her wrist.

Mum took her hand and turned it over under the lamp.

The scald ran from the bone on the outside of her wrist a thumb's length up her forearm, about as wide as a shoelace, the colour of a new penny, still wet down the middle.

"What's this?"

"The iron. Wednesday night."

Mum looked at the iron in its stand, and back at the wrist, and didn't let go. Her thumb stayed against the inside of Amara's arm, just under the scald, where it wasn't sore. "You burned yourself on Wednesday, and walked about in drain water on Thursday with it open, and didn't say."

"It's fine. It doesn't hurt."

"It's weeping." Mum let go and got the tin down from above the fridge, and put a dressing on it, square and white, pressing the edges flat with her fingers one side at a time. Amara held still for it. Mum had her meeting face on, the calm one, and her hands were too careful.

"Tuesday you're coming with me," Mum said.

"To the canal rooms?"

"I'm there till ten at least. I'm not having you down at that lake in the dark with your arm like this and nobody knowing where you are. You can sit at the back. Bring a book. Bring the box, if you like. There's a whole canal you can point it at."

Tuesday was meant to be the house to herself till eleven and the culvert with nobody asking. Now it was a plastic chair at the back of a room in town, an hour away, watching Mum say I hear you to people.

"Okay."

"Is that a yes? I want a yes."

"Yes."

Mum nodded, once, the way she did when a thing went in the diary. She put the papers back under her arm and drank half her tea, and kissed the top of Amara's head going past, and the front door went, and then the car.

Amara sat with the dressing on her wrist and the box in front of her. The needle still rested a hair off zero, where the pin had bent. On her phone Mum's third text from yesterday was still unanswered, and under it the three from Hyejin, still unanswered too. She opened Isabel's instead.

I've fixed the meter. Can you come out on Sunday and watch me hold it to the grate? I'll tell you before I move.

Isabel's flat was cold when they got back to it, and Hoshi was at the corner behind the desk, yowling at the join of the two walls. When Isabel came in the cat stopped, and looked at her, and turned back to the plaster and started again.

Natalie went straight to the sink and ran the cold tap. "Hand," she said.

Isabel gave it to her. Natalie peeled the gauze back with her fingertips, a corner at a time where it had stuck. Under it the scald across her knuckles had opened along its middle when she made the fist, and the long one had wept into it where they crossed. Natalie held the whole back of the hand under the water.

It was Natalie's right hand doing the holding, palm up under Isabel's, and across the base of it, over the callus, there was a scald Isabel hadn't seen in the lane. Copper-coloured, a thumb wide, raw at the edges.

"What's that?"

"Grate in Salter's Lane. The frost had it down in the iron."

"Did you get it?"

"No. It'll keep till there's a thaw." Natalie turned Isabel's hand a little so the water ran over the knuckles. "Say the thaw comes Tuesday. Five in the morning, Salter's Lane. How do you come at it?"

"From the top, not the bottom, because the bottom's always got the bins." Isabel thought. "And it'll be in the bars, not the stone. So you feel the iron first."

"And after that?"

Natalie's left hand had gone into her coat pocket while she asked, and something in there crackled, folded paper, the same sound as yesterday on the way out the door.

It was nearly the game from when Isabel was new, five years of walking behind Natalie at dawn and being asked what came next. Only then Natalie had never asked how Isabel would come at a thing, or said a day. "The Tangle's wall. The stall gutter, if it's been raining. Then the towpath, the two past the lock, the chapel yard, which freezes first, and the long one at the tram stop, which you do from the kerb or you get your feet wet. Then home."

Natalie turned the hand under the tap. "You've got it all. The whole round." Her other hand came out of the pocket empty.

So Isabel had passed. Five years, and one root gone under a building, and she was back to being asked the round like it was her first winter. Her phone had gone off on the walk back. She fished it out of her jacket left-handed and read it out over the sink, so Natalie would hear it.

I've fixed the meter. Can you come out on Sunday and watch me hold it to the grate? I'll tell you before I move.

She put it face down on the bench. "Amara. Full stops and all. I told her that yesterday, at the culvert, you know? Tell me before you move. It's the one thing I said that she listened to."

Natalie shut the tap off and patted Isabel's hand dry with the tea towel, round the scalds, not on them. "She's saying it, then," she said. "Somebody ought to be there to hear it."

Somebody. Yesterday in her kitchen it had been her cut and her jar. Now she was being let go to Linden Rise, like a schoolkid going on an excursion.

"I wasn't asking." She took the hand back. The words had come out harder than she'd meant, and she didn't take them back either. "I went out there yesterday on my own. Nobody had to say I could."

Natalie got clean gauze out of the chemist's bag and held out her hand, and waited until Isabel gave hers back.

"Say it, then," Isabel said. "This morning. Whatever it is."

Natalie started winding, round the knuckles and across the back and round the wrist, not too tight. "You felt it going on you and you didn't call me off the stair," she said. "You kept the staff on the wall. That's the thing I'd want from anybody."

"That's what lost it."

"It went either way."

"You don't know that."

"No," Natalie said. She tied off the gauze and tucked the end under. "Where's the jar?"

"Jacket."

"Keep the lid on it. She'll want it clean when you bring it."

Isabel looked at Natalie's wet trouser leg. The heater worked if you kicked it, and an hour on it would dry a sock, at least. She didn't offer it.

Natalie filled the kettle and switched it on, and got a mug down and put a teabag in it, one mug, and left it by the kettle for Isabel. She did up her coat over the wet trouser leg and went, and her boot squelched on every step of the stairs down.

The kettle came to the boil and clicked off. Isabel poured it, one-handed, and stood at the bench with it going cold, and got her phone up with her left thumb.

sunday, 10. don't go near the grate till i'm there.

She sent it. Behind the desk, at the cold corner, Hoshi drew breath and started again.

Chronicle

Natalie walked her Friday round from Weir Street at a quarter to five, through a hard frost. At Chandler's Row, Salter's Lane and the Bell Street drain she cleared small roots with her staff. The grate in Salter's Lane would not clear: the cold had driven the blight into the iron bars, and when she leaned on it the copper came back up her arms. Her right palm was scalded where she had pressed on the grate for about twelve minutes. She left the root there to wait for a thaw.

At the Tangle's wall she read the council notice in the stall window: a public consultation on a substrate hall, Tuesday 10 July, at the Canal Community Rooms. At the bridge she went down the bank toward the patch by the canal, slipped on the ice, and put her left boot into the water up to the shin. She came back up the bank on her hands and reopened the scald. She did the rest of the round with a wet, freezing trouser leg, and did not go home to change.

At Kell's Lane, Isabel opened the wall from the downpipe with her staff, held in her left hand, her right hand scalded and gauzed. Natalie waited under the fire stair. Before Isabel reached the stair, Natalie went in, and the end of the root she had pinned there went grey. The seam closed back along the wall behind Isabel, and the root ran into the drain at the foot of the downpipe and went under the building. Isabel had not called Natalie to the wall. She told Mrs Vella to keep the children off the downpipe and the drain.

At Rowan Crescent, Amara Nwosu repaired her meter on the kitchen table. She fixed a cold joint and reseated the needle, which now rests slightly high because the pin is bent. Her mother found the scald on her wrist and dressed it, then told her to attend the consultation evening on Tuesday. Amara agreed. She texted Isabel that she had mended the box and asked her to come on Sunday to watch her hold it to the grate.

Afterwards Natalie and Isabel went back to Isabel's flat. Natalie rinsed Isabel's hand under the cold tap and asked how she would come at the Salter's Lane grate once the thaw arrived, and then the rest of the round. Isabel answered. When Natalie said someone ought to be there for Amara on Sunday, Isabel told her she hadn't been asking. Natalie wound clean gauze round the hand, and left, having made one mug of tea. Isabel texted Amara that she should not go near the grate until Isabel was there.
